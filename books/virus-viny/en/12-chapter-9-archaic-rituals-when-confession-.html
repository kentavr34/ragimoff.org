Chapter 9. Archaic rituals: when confession becomes A weapon of control

“The best defense against evil is knowing how it works.” — Carl Jung

THE PUNISHMENT WE IMPOSE ON OURSELVES

Observe yourself on a day when you’ve made a mistake.

You skip the dessert you were looking forward to. You cancel plans with friends—“I don’t deserve it.” You work late for no reason. You talk to yourself in a tone you wouldn’t use even with an intern who had messed up. Look closely, and these aren’t random reactions. They’re a ritual. A precise, well-rehearsed ceremony of self-punishment, complete with its own logic, its own rules, and a history stretching back thousands of years.

Humanity has always sought ways to handle guilt—to displace it, atone for it, wash it away with pain, give it voice. Some of these rituals were wise, anticipating psychotherapy by millennia. Others became tools of control. And some still live inside us today—we just don’t call them rituals anymore. We say, “I’ll punish myself so I don’t make that mistake again.”

In this chapter, we’ll unpack archaic protocols for dealing with guilt—and see which ones are worth reclaiming, and which ones should have been retired long ago.

9.1 THE SCAPEGOAT: WHY WE SHIFT GUILT ONTO THE VICTIM

French philosopher and anthropologist René Girard (1923–2015), in his seminal work *The Scapegoat* (1982), proposed a model explaining why human societies have repeatedly returned to the concept of collective sacrifice.

THE SCAPEGOAT MECHANISM

A group of people is under strain. Someone has broken a rule. Or someone has fallen ill. Or the harvest has failed. The group needs an explanation and a way to release the tension. If guilt is spread across everyone, it becomes unbearable—each person feels their share of it. So the group instinctively singles out one member, usually someone weak, sick, or simply different, and declares them guilty of everything.

“Someone must be punished so that we can forgive one another.” — Jean Baudrillard

This isn’t just a metaphor. It is an actual social process. Research shows that communities that carry out the expulsion or execution of a scapegoat experience a temporary restoration of social cohesion. The tension lifts. But the price is horrifying: the truth stays buried, and no real mechanism for solving the problem ever develops.

THE PSYCHOLOGICAL RISK TO THE INDIVIDUAL

In modern psychology, the scapegoat mechanism plays out differently, but the core remains the same. A person refuses to take responsibility for their own feelings or mistakes, projecting them onto others instead. “You made me this way.” “If you hadn’t said that, I wouldn’t have lost control.” It’s an attempt to become an “innocent bystander” in one’s own tragedy. The guilt doesn’t disappear—it simply shifts from one set of shoulders to another. Ultimately, the person is left alone with themselves, because no one else is truly responsible for their state.

CLINICAL BOX How do you spot projected guilt? If your client is constantly blaming outside forces (“my boss drove me crazy,” “my spouse ruined my life”), ask yourself: “What exactly is he doing about his own life in response?” Projection paralyzes action. Taking responsibility—even partial responsibility—restores a sense of power. The therapeutic task here is to help the client reclaim authorship of their life, without casting blame but also without denying the reality of the situation.

9.2 SELF-FLAGELLATION: ENDORPHINS OR PUNISHMENT?

One of humanity’s most universal guilt-relief strategies throughout history has been self-inflicted pain. Medieval European flagellants, Hindu ascetics in India, modern self-harming teenagers—all follow the same logic: “If I hurt myself, God (or conscience, or my brain) will forgive me.”

THE NEUROBIOLOGICAL EXPLANATION

The mechanism is straightforward. Acute pain activates the body’s endorphin system. Endorphins are the brain’s natural opioids. Once the sharp pain passes, relief follows—anxiety drops, sometimes giving way to euphoria. To the brain, this is a signal: “The danger is over. You’ve been forgiven.”

• Short-term effect: a feeling of being “cleansed,” a temporary drop in anxiety.

• Long-term effect: the neural association between pain and relief becomes entrenched.

THE PARADOX OF SELF-FLAGELLATION

When people grow accustomed to relieving guilt through pain, they lose touch with other ways of regulating their emotional state. This creates a risk of addiction—not to a substance, but to the sensation of “redemption through suffering.” It is one of the mechanisms that link chronic guilt to eating disorders, anorexia, and depression.

Consider the flagellant movement of the fourteenth century, which reached its peak during the plague epidemics. People flogged themselves in public, hoping to appease God. The logic was simple: “God is punishing us for our sins. If we show Him how much we hurt, He will see that we are sincere and forgive us.”

Psychologically, this is a catastrophic mistake. Guilt is not a commodity you can buy with suffering. It demands understanding and a change in behavior. Pain only masks it temporarily.

9.3 CONFESSION: A REMINDER

We examined the psychology of confession in detail in Chapter 5—its four mechanisms of action and its inherent contradiction. To recap briefly: verbalization reduces emotional intensity, an authoritative listener legitimizes the experience, the ritual creates a clear “before/after” boundary, and the community alleviates isolation. Yet the ritual merely addresses symptoms without challenging the core belief “I am sinful”—which is why it demands endless repetition. This framework will prove useful as we now turn to another ritual that harnessed the same power differently.

9.4 The Patient Who Wore a Hairshirt

Before we continue our tour through the museum of rituals, let me share a case from my practice that convinced me this particular museum has never shut its doors.

Emil, forty-six, CFO. Three years ago, his miscalculation cost the company a substantial sum. No one was fired; the owner forgave him, and the matter was closed. For everyone but Emil.

Since then, he’d adopted a private regimen even his wife didn’t know about. He woke at 4:30 AM—“only layabouts sleep in.” Rain or shine, sick or well, he ran—illness wasn’t an excuse. He fasted until noon. Canceled vacations—three years straight. Bought a watch cheaper than he could afford: “Haven’t earned better.” And every night before shutting his laptop, he triple-checked the day’s figures—not out of diligence, but like reciting a prayer, word for word.

When I read his own schedule back to him out loud, he gave a wry smile: “Discipline.” So I told him about the flagellants—people in the fourteenth century who marched along the roads of Europe whipping themselves, believing that voluntary pain would erase sin and stop the plague. Emil listened in silence. Then he said something I wrote down: “So I’m doing exactly the same thing. Only instead of a scourge, I have an alarm clock.”

He didn’t feel better because he had “punished himself enough”—enough is never enough. He felt better the first time he asked: who handed down the sentence? And it turned out that for three years, the judge, the prosecutor, and the executioner had all been the same person. Himself.

Self-flagellation didn’t die out in the fourteenth century. It just upgraded its tools.

9.5 YOM KIPPUR: THE OLDEST PSYCHOTHERAPY PROTOCOL ON RECORD

The Jewish Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) stands as one of history’s most elaborate guilt-processing rituals. Unlike many other religions, Judaism provides a concrete procedure structurally aligned with modern cognitive-behavioral therapy.

THE PROCEDURE INVOLVES FOUR STEPS (ACCORDING TO MAIMONIDES):

Confession — verbal and sincere. The person puts what they did into their own words.

Remorse — genuine distress over what was done.

Cessation — immediately stopping the offending behavior.

Commitment — a firm resolve not to repeat it in the future.

Each of these steps corresponds to what modern psychology considers an essential component of working with guilt: verbalization (step one), the affective component (step two), the behavioral component (step three), and the cognitive component (step four). Maimonides wrote this in the twelfth century — without any reference to psychology, which did not yet exist.

One principle is especially important when it comes to sins against other people: Yom Kippur—the Day of Atonement—atones only for sins against God. Sins against others can be atoned for only through genuine reconciliation with the people harmed.

“No forgiveness from God without reconciliation with others.” — Rabbinic tradition

This is a crucial distinction from the Christian concept—or at least some versions of it—where inner repentance can be considered sufficient even without any outward action. Yom Kippur makes one thing clear: you cannot do real work on guilt without repairing your relationships with other people. It is one of the few ancient rituals fully supported by modern science.

9.6 RELIGIOUS OCCULTISM: WHEN PSYCHOLOGY TURNS INTO MAGIC

The modern world is seeing a resurgence of interest in mystical practices for relieving guilt. Forgiveness meditations, “karma cleansing” programs, energy healing sessions promising instant liberation—these are often marketed as psychological aid.

THE PSYCHOLOGICAL MECHANISM

The mechanism here is exactly the same as it was in the age of indulgences: hand over your money (or your energy, or your time) and receive relief. The problem is that such practices rarely touch the client’s core beliefs. They operate on the surface level of emotion while leaving the deep-seated conviction “I am unworthy” completely intact.

THE PHENOMENON OF SYMPTOMATIC TREATMENT

A client comes in asking to “get rid of feelings of guilt.” He is offered a relaxation exercise, a meditation on light, the chanting of mantras. A week later, the client reports, “I feel calmer.” But at the very first mistake, the old reaction returns. Why? Because the practice targeted the symptom, not the cause. The cause was the belief “I am not good enough.” The symptom was anxiety. Anxiety can be numbed. Changing a belief takes years of therapy.

9.7 CLINICAL EXAMPLES: WHY SOME APPROACHES FAIL

Let’s return to the people whose stories run through this book to see how these mechanisms play out in real life.

MARIA’S STORY

Maria, our subject from earlier chapters, was raised in a strict religious environment. When her marriage began failing, she turned to prayer seeking forgiveness. It provided temporary relief—but the underlying issue remained. Only when she realized her struggle wasn’t about sin before God, but about violated personal boundaries, did things improve. Prayer stopped being a guilt-dissolving tool and became instead a source of spiritual nourishment.

ANNA’S STORY

Anna, a manager plagued by chronic stomach pain, had tried every relaxation technique out there. One day she attempted a relaxation program built around meditating on “light.” She felt better. But after an argument with her colleagues, the pain came back. So she tried a practice of acknowledgment—much like the one used on Yom Kippur: she wrote down exactly what had happened, what she had felt, and what could be changed. The pain subsided. Why? Because she shifted from magical thinking to dealing with reality.

THE KEY TAKEAWAY: Archaic rituals can be useful, provided they offer a structure aimed at changing behavior. But if they promise illusory purification without requiring any accountability, they become a trap.

9.8 BRIDGING TO THE PRESENT: WHAT DO WE DO WITH THIS KNOWLEDGE?

This brings us to the central question of the chapter: what good is this ancient knowledge to us today?

First, we must acknowledge that the need for purification rituals is natural. Our brains crave closure. Simply forgetting about a problem isn’t enough—we need to go through a process of acknowledgment.

Second, we must reject formal, empty rituals that yield no real results. Donating to charity instead of actually working on repairing relationships? That’s a mask. Genuine forgiveness requires effort and time.

Third, we need the support of a community. As the Yom Kippur research showed, social acceptance eases guilt. Isolation deepens it. That is exactly why mutual support groups, therapy groups, and healthy friendships are among the most powerful tools we have for preventing depression.

PRACTICAL EXERCISE: CREATE YOUR OWN CLEANSING RITUAL

1. Name the problem. Write down on a piece of paper exactly what is triggering your sense of guilt. 2. Describe the emotions. What do you feel in your body? Where do you feel it? 3. Set boundaries. Acknowledge this: it isn’t your guilt; it is someone else’s responsibility. 4. Take a step. Write a letter (you don’t have to send it) apologizing, if you were in the wrong. Or write a letter to yourself, if the guilt isn’t yours to carry. 5. Close the loop. Read it out loud, then burn it or delete the file as a symbol that the process is complete.

This simple protocol brings together the ancient wisdom of acknowledgment and our modern understanding of neuropsychology.

CHAPTER SUMMARY

We’ve come a long way from sacrificial rituals to spreadsheets. Yet the same challenge persists: how to live with guilt without destroying ourselves. Ancient rituals showed us that we are capable of greatness when we acknowledge our mistakes and make amends. But they also taught us a dangerous illusion: that guilt can be “cried out” or “released” without taking real action.

Now we have history. Now we understand the mechanisms of the past. But the most important part still lies ahead: practical methods that can help you transform yourself right now. The next chapter, “Evidence-Based Therapy,” will open the doors to modern psychotherapy offices and introduce tools grounded in decades of scientific research. From CBT to CFT, it will all be available to you in the next chapter.

CHAPTER SUMMARY

Scapegoating. Shifting guilt onto a victim offers temporary relief, but it doesn’t solve the problem.

Self-flagellation. Pain triggers an endorphin release, but it creates an addiction to suffering.

Confession. Only works when four levels are present: verbalization, legitimization, completion, belonging.

Yom Kippur. An ancient protocol that structurally mirrors modern therapy (acknowledgment → remorse → cessation → resolution).

Rituals today. They are useful if they aim to change behavior rather than achieve magical purification.

Practice. Creating a personal closure ritual helps the brain finally close the gestalt of guilt.
