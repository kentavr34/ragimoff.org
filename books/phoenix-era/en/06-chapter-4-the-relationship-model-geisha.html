Chapter 4. The relationship model · “GEISHA”

The art of self-expression: The trap of public admiration.

1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The name of the model comes from the Japanese word “Geisha” (芸者), where “gei” means art and “sha” means person. Literally: “a person of art.” In Japanese culture, this is the image of a woman who entertains guests with her talent yet remains untouchable.

If we follow the logic of the historical context, this is not simply a profession — it is a philosophy of service through excellence. Unlike a prostitute, a Geisha sells not her body, but emotion, atmosphere, the illusion of perfection. In Greek mythology, echoes of this archetype can be seen in the figures of the Muses — goddesses who inspired creators but rarely became their wives.

The roots of the image go back to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Historically, the first geishas were men — entertainers of the samurai (hōkan). Later, women replaced them. The key figure was Kikuya, a former courtesan who had won her freedom. She established a rule: a Geisha may enchant, but she is not obligated to belong physically. This divided them into “white” geisha (art only) and korobi geisha (the “falling” ones, who permitted intimacy). In the context of our methodology, “Geisha” is the priority of public recognition and talent over personal attachment.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

People in the “Geisha” state of mind often broadcast thoughts that become their manifesto. Here are the three core beliefs characteristic of this model:

“My talent demands sacrifices, and love is the first of them.” (A justification of loneliness in the name of success.)

“The admiration of the crowd matters more than the love of one.” (Public recognition takes priority over intimacy.)

“Reputation is the one thing you cannot afford to lose.” (Fear of criticism and of losing one’s image.)

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

What happens in a person’s life story when this model takes shape? Often it is compensation for an inferiority complex by reaching the very top. The person decides that their worth must be proven to the public. They begin attracting attention not through availability (as Puttana does), but through unavailability and mastery.

In psychoanalytic terms, what happens here is the sublimation of libido into creativity. If the Id demands pleasure, the Geisha transforms that demand into applause. She is ready to sacrifice personal happiness for the stage. In their youth, such people may seem inspired, but in maturity they often face loneliness surrounded by admirers. They do not build deep bonds, because they fear that someone close will see them as “imperfect.”

Psychological mechanism: A person discovers they possess a talent or charisma that draws admiration. The psyche adapts: to feel safe, they begin to depend on external evaluation. A cognitive distortion arises: “I am loved only for my success/image.” This grows into a life philosophy. They start to see themselves as “the chosen one,” obliged to shine for others. Thus the need for love is replaced by the need for worship.

The crisis of choice: Career or Love? The methodology describes the Geisha’s central conflict. When a partner appears who demands exclusivity, the Geisha faces a choice: the crowd or one person. The historical example of King Edward VIII, who renounced the throne for love, is a rare exception. Far more often, the Geisha chooses the career. If she sacrifices her reputation for love, she moves into the Cinderella model (serving one person). If she preserves her image but loses touch with her feelings, she may slide into the Chameleon (adjusting to the public) or, if her reputation is damaged, into Lilith (money instead of fame).

Literary and mythological context: Recall the story of Kikuya, the first female geisha. Having won her freedom, she refused to sell her body, selling only the art of conversation. It was a rebellion against the system — and at the same time, the building of a new cage. In the methodology, this serves as a metaphor for the price of perfection: a person can look great on the outside while carrying inside the emptiness of a restless soul. Like the samurai who sharpens his sword for years, forgetting what the war was for, the Geisha sharpens her image, forgetting what love was for.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Geisha” model — talent, service to the public, priority of image over personal life.

Maria Callas (Greece/USA, 20th century).

Her voice was a weapon capable of breaking hearts, yet her own heart remained a fortress.

Manifestations of the model: The greatest opera singer, whose life became a textbook example of choosing art over personal happiness. Her relationship with Aristotle Onassis revealed the Geisha’s eternal conflict: when she had to choose between the stage and her lover, she hesitated — and in the end lost both. Her existence was devoted to inspiring admiration through her voice, even at the cost of her health and her solitude. She died alone, leaving behind a legend, but not a family.

Coco Chanel (France, 20th century).

She created the style of an era, yet remained outside time — and outside anyone’s embrace.

Manifestations of the model: A fashion designer who built an empire of style and became a symbol of independence. Her romances repeatedly took a back seat to her work. She created the image of a woman admired by millions, yet she herself admitted to loneliness. Her life demonstrates the priority of legacy and reputation over a traditional family. She became a brand in her own lifetime, paying for it with the right to ordinary weakness.

Marlene Dietrich (Germany/USA, 20th century).

She controlled the stage lights so carefully that no one would ever see the shadow in her eyes.

Manifestations of the model: An actress and singer who managed her image with meticulous care. Famous for the line: “I dress for the image, not for the man.” She directed men’s attention, but never allowed them to direct her. Her life was a performance in which she always remained the “untouchable star,” even in intimate relationships, preserving the distance of the Geisha.

Sarah Bernhardt (France, 19th–20th centuries).

Her death was staged as carefully as her life.

Manifestations of the model: The great actress, christened “the Divine Sarah,” renowned for her theatrical gestures and a life lived on display. She turned her life into a show. Even her death was choreographed. Bernhardt embodied a model where the public matters more than family. She kept male lovers, but her true love remained the stage. Her existence confirms the thesis: the Geisha belongs to art, not to a person.

Madonna (USA, 21st century).

The queen of the pop stage, who keeps changing masks in order to stay loved.

Manifestations of the model: A pop icon who endlessly reinvents her image to hold the public’s attention. Her career is built on the ability to provoke admiration and shock. Her personal relationships often became material for her art. She represents the modern version of the Geisha: the constant upkeep of an image demands the sacrifice of privacy. Her life illustrates the conviction: “The admiration of the crowd is a drug you cannot live without.”

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

A person may face loneliness — or fight to protect their reputation. When the supply of admiration runs dry (age, a shift in the public’s taste), the crisis begins.

The depressive exit: The person realizes: “They applaud me, but they don’t love me.” The Geisha withdraws into herself. If she decides that love matters more than fame, she may move to the Cinderella model (devotion to one person) or the Chameleon (adapting to a partner). If she decides the world will never appreciate her, she may retreat into the Hippie (a rejection of standards).

The aggressive exit: The person rages against criticism. “They don’t deserve my talent.” The Geisha begins attacking the haters, defending her reputation. If the reputation is hopelessly damaged, she may shift to the Lilith model (monetizing the image, money instead of fame) or to the Puttana (shock for attention, at any price).

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “GEISHA”

Two years had passed since that night in the cold house. Maria accepted David’s help, but not the way he had hoped: she did not become his woman. First she became his project, and then — her own. She moved to the city, rented a small studio, and started a blog about the life of a “former rebel.” She turned her story into content.

The first year swept by on a wave of euphoria. Her followers loved her sincerity. She posted photos with books, quotes, her own paintings; she was invited on podcasts, and she once again felt that same current of admiration she had missed so badly in the Hippie model. There she had been free, but invisible; here, she was in full view. Every morning began with the phone: how many new followers, how many comments, who wrote, who shared — and on those numbers hinged what kind of day it would be. David sometimes dropped by, brought groceries, and looked at her with hope.

On one of those days Maria was getting ready for yet another shoot: she adjusted the collar of her blouse, checked the fold, set the lights. The perfect fold. The perfect light. And then David, standing in the doorway with a bag in his hands, asked quietly, without reproach:

“Tell me honestly — aren’t you lonely?”

Maria froze, and an inner dialogue switched on in her head at once — cold and calculating, like an algorithm:

“If I tell the truth, I lose control. If I admit I’m lonely, the magic vanishes. A Geisha is never lonely — she is merely unavailable. I create an image so I won’t have to feel the pain.”

Psychologists would call this defense through idealization: the flawless picture isn’t for the audience — it’s for the person herself, so she never has to face her own pain. And Maria never did tell the truth.

David shook his head. In his eyes she saw not anger, but weariness.

He left, closing the door softly behind him, and Maria was alone. A notification flashed on her phone screen: “+1000 followers.” She felt a sting of joy — brief, sharp, like a drug — and David’s tired gaze instantly retreated to the far edge of memory: the number on the screen proved louder.

Six more months passed, and Maria found herself at the peak. She was invited to speak, quoted everywhere, and wore beautiful dresses she could never have afforded even in the era of Lilith. But in the evenings, when the stream of likes died down, the studio grew quiet. Too quiet. The dresses hung in the closet like costumes in a theater dressing room, and Maria caught herself more and more often not knowing which one was actually hers.

One day, after a talk, a young man approached her — handsome, clearly successful — and, a little embarrassed, asked her out on a date.

Maria smiled the same practiced smile she’d rehearsed in front of the mirror.

“My managers will get in touch with you.”

She said “managers,” though she had no managers at all. She said it to keep her distance: a Geisha doesn’t go on dates, a Geisha receives admirers.

Back home, she took off her shoes — her feet ached after the long evening — and walked over to the mirror. A woman looked back at her, a woman loved by thousands. But when Maria pressed her palm against the glass, the reflection stayed cold.

“Why do I feel empty?” she asked herself. “I’ve made it. I’m safe. I’m valued. But value is an assessment from the outside, and love is a connection from the inside. I built a temple and forgot to leave a place in it for myself. I’ve become an exhibit in my own museum.”

She sat down on the floor right there in her expensive dress and thought of David — his simple hands, his quiet voice. He had offered her warmth, and she had chosen the glare of spotlights. But light warms only from the outside, and inside, she was cold.

Her phone buzzed on the table. A message from David: “I saw your talk. You were beautiful. But you looked very lonely. The door is still open — but not forever.”

Maria stared at the screen, and a tear slid down her cheek, smudging her makeup. For the first time in two years, she didn’t reach for a tissue to dab it away neatly — she let the mascara run. It was unbecoming, nothing like the script. But it was real.

She understood: to be a Geisha means to stand on stage forever, and from the stage you can’t see the faces in the audience. All she could see were the glows of phone screens, and she wanted to see eyes. One single pair of eyes — no applause.

That night she didn’t post a single photo, and for the first time in two years her feed stayed empty. She sat in the dark and wondered: “What if I step out of the role? What if I allow myself to be ordinary? Not perfect — just alive?”

But the fear was stronger. If she took off the mask, who would be left underneath? Maria was afraid the answer was no one, and that fear kept her in the Geisha model a while longer. Yet the crack had already appeared, and light was breaking through it — not the light of the stage, but the light of the most ordinary morning.
