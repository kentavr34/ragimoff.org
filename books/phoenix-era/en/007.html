Safety Guarantee: The Trap of Dependency.

1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The “Mistress” model describes a woman afraid of loneliness and seeking someone to lean on. She falls into emotional and psychological dependency, even while finding pleasure in love. It is the image of a woman who has a relationship outside marriage with a married person.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

Stereotypical thinking: “With him, my life is guaranteed; I love him deeply.” The model’s slogan: “Whatever happens, he must be mine.”

Within our method, the “Mistress” relationship model is not simply the status of a woman involved in an extramarital affair; it is a deep psychological archetype of dependency. It is a model in which a person, most often a woman, feels an acute need to be under someone’s care, sacrificing her own autonomy for the illusion of security. At the heart of the Mistress’s thinking lies a stereotype: “With him, my life is guaranteed.” This belief translates into an unspoken contract: “I love him, so I’ll forgive everything,” or, more categorically: “He can’t live without me. I’m his salvation.” The main driving force here is not material gain, as with Lilith, but the fear of loneliness. The Mistress is willing to endure difficult, sometimes humiliating conditions, if only she isn’t left alone. She clings to her partner like a life preserver, even when that preserver has long since turned into an anchor dragging her to the bottom.

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

This kind of cognitive distortion in her thinking partly resembles the “Lilith” relationship model, but the Mistress’s thinking does not put a direct sexual connection front and center. Instead, by adorning the relationship with spiritual values (love, union of souls, compatibility of zodiac signs and other mystical notions), she tries to fortify her bond with her patron through emotional ties. So, unlike “Lilith,” the “Mistress” can meet her needs at her beloved’s expense even when she is given no money or other material resources. For example, “Lilith,” by giving her beloved a feeling of pity, receives from him a certain financial allowance, but in the hours when she isn’t seeing him, she enjoys freedom of choice in her lifestyle and sexual relationships. The “Mistress,” however, by giving her beloved that same feeling of pity, in return always acts according to his needs and is obliged to remain sexually faithful to him. Otherwise, since no difference would remain between her and “Lilith,” her beloved, refusing to meet her needs, could replace her with another woman in no time at all.

Unlike the Lilith model, where the relationship is built on an outright trade of the body for resources and where the woman keeps her freedom outside their meetings, the Mistress strives for an emotional tether. She wraps the dependency in romantic images: destiny, kindred souls, a karmic connection. This keeps her from feeling like she’s selling herself, even though the essence of the exchange stays the same: independence in exchange for support and protection. Lilith can have several partners and manage her time freely, whereas the Mistress is obligated to stay faithful to her patron, because her security depends solely on his favor.

The psychological root of this model often traces back to infantilism. This is an adult with a child’s mind, searching for a “parent” in a partner. The question “What will I do if I’m left alone?” plays in her head like constant background noise. The “Mistress” doesn’t want to take responsibility for her own life, for her own earnings, for her own decisions. It’s easier for her to find someone who will decide everything for her. Often this person is considerably older, filling the role of a surrogate parent. But even if the partner is her own age, the dynamic stays the same: he is the source of resources and decisions, she is the recipient, obliged to pay for it with attention, care, and sexual exclusivity.

In society, the image of the Mistress is often criticized, but her magnetism lies in the way she offers a person the feeling of being needed and powerful. She doesn’t demand titles openly, the way the Geisha does; she demands warmth and presence. Yet this model conceals an enormous danger for the woman herself. Because her status is precarious and depends on another person’s desire, she lives in constant tension. Any threat of a breakup is perceived as a catastrophe.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Mistress” model: emotional dependency, the search for a guarantor of security, a life lived in the partner’s shadow.

Madame de Maintenon (France, 17th century).

The widow of Scarron, who became a governess and then the secret wife of Louis XIV.

Manifestation of the model: She never demanded titles openly, but she secured her safety through total devotion to the king. Her life shows a transition from dependency to influence, yet the foundation was built on the role of “savior” and keeper of the hearth for the monarch, which is typical of the highest form of the Mistress model.

Camille Claudel (France, 19th–20th centuries).

A sculptor, the muse and lover of Auguste Rodin.

Manifestation of the model: She sacrificed her own creative work for a place in Rodin’s workshop, hoping for reciprocity and recognition through him. Her dependency on the master became destructive. When the relationship cooled, she could not find support within herself and ended her life in an asylum. The tragedy of a talent that dissolved into her partner.

Wallis Simpson (USA/UK, 20th century), before the marriage.

The American woman for whom a king gave up the throne.

Manifestation of the model: Until the moment of abdication, she, herself a married woman, held the position of mistress to Prince Edward. Her grip on the prince’s attention through emotional dependency and her status as “the only one who understands him” led to a global consequence, but the behavioral model itself was a classic one: safety through the exclusivity of the bond.

Marie Duplessis (France, 19th century).

The prototype of The Lady of the Camellias (Alexandre Dumas), a courtesan who sought protection.

Manifestation of the model: Despite her status as a courtesan, her psychology gravitated toward the Mistress model: to find one man who would take her out of the world of selling her body. Her dependency on Count Perregaux and the illness that followed show the collapse of the hope of being saved by a man.

Anna Karenina (a literary archetype, L. Tolstoy).

A society lady who left her husband for a lover.

Manifestation of the model: A perfect example of the model’s collapse. Anna transferred the entire meaning of her life onto Vronsky. When he began to live his own life (his service, his social circle), she could not bear the loss of being “the only one.” Her suicide is the result of realizing that without her “guarantor,” her life is empty. This is the method’s warning: you must never make one person the sole source of your safety.

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

The crisis of the Mistress model arrives inevitably. Sooner or later the partner’s sexual interest fades, or the lawful wife finds out about the affair, or the Mistress herself grows older, losing her trump card: her looks. At that moment she finds herself in a vacuum. She has no resources of her own, no survival skills, no Plan B. She feels like a leech stranded on the shore of a dried-up lake.

At this critical point, two paths of development open up, two ways of exiting the model.

The depressive exit: When the Mistress realizes that her patron has left and no new one is coming, she sinks into the state of a victim. She starts complaining to everyone around her about the injustice of fate, about the callousness of men, about her miserable lot. Her demands drop; she is ready to settle for crumbs as long as someone pays attention to her. At this point she transforms into the Beggar model. She no longer demands guarantees; she asks for help, courting pity. This state is often accompanied by psychosomatic illnesses: the body responds to stress with kidney, skin, and immune disorders. She lives in constant expectation of a savior who never comes, and gradually sinks into chronic depression. If she cannot find a new donor, she lowers her expectations of provision, which only makes her more vulnerable and more dependent on the mercy of others.

The aggressive exit: If the Mistress feels deceived, if she believes her partner stole the best years of her life, rage awakens in her. She decides to take revenge. Blackmail comes into play: threats to tell the wife, to show up at his workplace, to expose intimate details. She may reach out to television shows, post on social media, try to destroy the life of her former patron. This is the phase of open war. Sometimes, if the scandal becomes public and an audience of sympathizers gathers around her, she can transform into the Geisha, using her fame and her story of suffering as a stage. But most often the aggressive phase ends in courtrooms, police stations, and an even deeper fall into depression. This public conflict sometimes changes the mindset of the woman carrying the model, pushing her to share her life tragedy with people, gathering followers around her pain. This can become a springboard for a move into the Geisha model, where she promises never to sacrifice her freedom again.

It is important to understand that the Mistress model is not always tied to official status. Even a legal wife can think like a Mistress if her only goal is being provided for by her husband, with no personal development whatsoever. If the husband can no longer handle the role of provider, such a marriage falls apart, because the foundation of safety is destroyed. This is a dead-end branch of personal evolution, where a person refuses to grow, preferring comfort to development.

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “MISTRESS”

She never did answer David. For a week she carried the unsent message around in her phone, then another week, and after that answering became awkward, and she told herself he had probably stopped waiting. The blog she abandoned that same winter. Writing the old way was no longer possible, writing the truth frightened her, and her readers, after waiting a while, drifted off to others.

Six months had passed since her last post when she met Omar. He was twenty years older, with soft lines around his eyes and the calm of people who long ago stopped looking at price tags. They met at someone’s anniversary party, to which she had still been invited for old times’ sake. He didn’t know who she was, hadn’t read a single post of hers, and never took out his phone all evening; he only asked why she wasn’t eating and moved half of his fish onto her plate.

He had no use for her confessions or her pretty photographs. He wanted the house he came to to be quiet and someone in it to be glad to see him. Maria listened to him and, for the first time in two years, exhaled. A month later she moved into his country house and left the studio, lamps and dresses and all, just as it was.

Omar told her right away that he was married. “It’s complicated there,” he added, looking off to the side, and she didn’t press him. She wanted to believe, and she believed.

The first year went by the way a long sleep does after insomnia. She got up late, walked in the garden, learned to cook what he liked, ironed his shirts even though the house had staff, and in the evenings listened to him talk about warehouses, shipments, and dishonest partners, understanding nothing and nodding in the right places. Once he fell asleep in the middle of his own story with his head in her lap, and she sat for two hours without moving, her legs numb, perfectly happy: here was a man who felt at peace beside her. The days were as alike as beads, and the thread that held them was his arrival. The account where her advertising money used to come in she hadn’t checked since spring.

“With him, my life is guaranteed.” That is the conviction the Mistress model rests on: security received from another person takes the place of a life of one’s own, and for the sake of it everything is forgiven in advance.

Little by little her whole life narrowed down to the phone. Omar called around seven, and by seven she could no longer read or go for a walk. She didn’t leave the house unless she had to: what if he came and she wasn’t there? When he forgot her birthday, she made up an excuse for him before he could have made one up himself, and all evening she was especially tender so that he wouldn’t feel guilty.

“He gets tired. He has so many people depending on him. I’m the one place where nobody asks him questions. He’d be worse off without me.”

She did ask one question, once, at dinner. She had been preparing for a week, and what came out was nothing like the words she had prepared:

“Omar, what’s going to happen to us later on?”

She laid down her fork, and its clink against the plate sounded deafening to her. Omar chewed for a long time. Then he looked at her with pity or with fatigue, she couldn’t tell which, and said:

“Let’s not do this tonight.”

She never asked again. It seemed to her that every such question brought the thing she feared closer.

Another year and a half went by, and he began coming late. If there was no call by eight, her temples started to pound. While he was in the shower she went through the pockets of his jacket; before doing the laundry she sniffed the collars of his shirts and despised herself for it, and the next day she checked again. She always met him with a smile and said nothing about his being late. One night, after he had fallen asleep, she picked up his phone, found the number saved as “Home,” and copied it into her own. Why, she didn’t know; it simply made her calmer to have it.

In November he didn’t come at all, and his phone was switched off. Maria sat in the living room until midnight with the lights off so she could see headlights on the drive, and the house that had so long seemed a fortress was showing her its other side. Nothing here was hers: no money that she knew of, no friends she could comfortably call after so many years of silence. There was him, and if he were gone, what would be left of her was the shirts in the closet.

Sometime after midnight she dialed the number saved as “Home.” It was picked up on the third ring.

“Hello,” said a woman’s voice, no longer young, sleepy. “Hello, who is this? Omar, is that you?”

Maria hung up and sat for a long time holding the phone in both hands. So he wasn’t sleeping there either. So that woman was sitting up and waiting too. The thought should have comforted her, and instead she was ashamed, the way you are as a child when you’ve listened at a door.

He came in the morning, gray-faced and red-eyed, and stopped in the middle of the living room as if in a stranger’s house.

“We need to talk.”

She knew that tone: it’s how people speak when everything has already been decided.

An envelope landed on the table, thick and heavy. Maria looked at it and thought that the envelopes in her life kept getting thicker while she herself kept getting smaller. It was enough money to live on for half a year, maybe a year, and what came after was frightening to think about.

“You used me,” she said, and her voice broke.

“No. I loved you. But it can’t go on like this. Not with you, and not with her.”

Whether he knew about the call in the night, she never found out. He stood there a little longer, seemed about to touch her shoulder, and didn’t. The door closed.

“If I give the money back, I’ll be proud and penniless. If I take it, then all of this was a transaction. But I did love him. Or did I love being able, with him, not to be afraid of anything?”

No answer came to her. She sat on the couch where he had once fallen asleep in her lap, holding the envelope in both hands, and there were no tears. She remembered the studio with its lamps, remembered the morning numbers: they had been fake, but at least they had been her own. Here, for almost three years, she had been an add-on to someone else’s life, and now the add-on had been returned.

In the morning she packed her things. The envelope she took: she had to live on something, and pride, as it turned out, costs money too. On the drive she stopped and typed to David: “Is the door still open?” She stood looking at the screen and pictured him asking where she had been all this time and why she had thought of him only now. Then she erased what she had written and called a taxi.

“The airport.”

In the car she promised herself never again to wait at a window. From now on, let them wait for her.
