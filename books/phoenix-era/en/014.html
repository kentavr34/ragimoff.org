1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The model takes its name from a living creature — the chameleon. This reptile can change the color of its skin to match its surroundings, becoming invisible — or, on the contrary, strikingly bright — whenever it pays to do so. In the context of our method, the “Chameleon” is a person who has mastered the art of adaptation but lost their own color.

From the standpoint of evolutionary psychology, this is the highest form of social conformity. It is a transitional stage. To reach the level of the Phoenix, a person inevitably passes through the stage of the Chameleon. It is the moment when someone, having sampled every neurotic model, realizes that not one of them delivers wholeness. They begin trying each on for size, becoming convenient for everyone — but a stranger to themselves.

The roots of the image lie in politics and diplomacy. In the terminology of the method, the “Chameleon” means placing external success above inner sincerity. It is a model in which a person speaks the language of their interlocutor — and forgets their own.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

People in the “Chameleon” state of mind often broadcast thoughts that conceal their inner emptiness. Here are the three core beliefs typical of this model:

“With every person, you have to speak their language.” (The belief that sincerity is less effective than adaptation).

“I have everything, and yet something is missing.” (A sense of existential emptiness against the backdrop of outward success).

“A person comes into this world alone and leaves it alone.” (Justifying inner loneliness with a philosophy of fatalism).

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

The path through chaos toward adaptation. A person often begins their journey with infantile dependence (the Infanta), then tries to buy safety (Lilith, the Mistress). They rebel against the system (the Puttana, the Hippie), try to conquer the world with talent (the Geisha, the Courtesan), or sacrifice themselves for others (Teresa, Cinderella). Sometimes they fall to the very bottom, demanding help (the Beggar).

If we follow the logic of the source, all of these models are neurotic. They are built on false needs. When each one comes crashing down, they find themselves searching. They start looking for a way out. And the first solution they find is adaptation. They understand: to survive, you must be convenient. In this way they come one step closer to the Chameleon.

Psychological mechanism: The Chameleon has a broad outlook. They know that different peoples hold different values, and they accept it. “Everyone has their own opinion, and everyone deserves respect” — that is their favorite phrase. They avoid conflict, because they read another person’s mood masterfully. In any company, they are a welcome guest. They get invited to events; they succeed in their career, especially in politics or diplomacy.

In transactional analysis terms, this is a block on one’s own Self.

The Chameleon does not know what they themselves want. They confuse their own desires with the desires of those around them. On the outside they look understanding; on the inside they suffer from loneliness. They ask themselves: “I don’t know what I want. Why do I live?”

Loneliness in a crowd. This is the key marker of the model. A person may be surrounded by friends, family, and colleagues — and still feel like a stranger. “I have everything — work, family, friends — and yet something is missing, as if there’s some unknown need” — that is the Chameleon’s typical complaint. Unlike other models, where a person suffers from a lack of resources or love, the Chameleon suffers from a lack of self.

The transition to the Phoenix: The Moment of Truth. How does a Chameleon become a Phoenix? For that, an inner upheaval is needed. A person must understand that adaptation is not the way. They must find their Mission. To illustrate, let’s take Miyamoto Musashi. The great Japanese samurai of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, a master of the sword. He won every duel — and came to understand that strength does not lie in the sword. Legend has it that once, unarmed, fending off attackers with an oar, he realized: mastery lives within, not in the instrument. He burned his old knowledge and founded a new school. So it is with the Chameleon. When they find a value that matters more than the crowd’s opinion, they burn their masks. They stop adjusting — and start leading.

The Phoenix is the final destination. The Phoenix is a mature personality. This is not a myth; it is a psychological reality. A person who has burned their neurotic models in the fire of awareness and been reborn in sincerity. They do not manipulate — they love. Their need is not safety or success, but connection and love for the sake of the process itself. This is the level where a person cares for others not for the sake of approval (as Teresa does), but because it is their nature. Like the Phoenix bird in the legend of Noah’s Ark, which asked for no food so as not to trouble Noah — and for that was granted eternal life. It is a symbol of mercy and independence.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Chameleon” model — adaptability, success, but a search for an inner mission.

Charles Maurice de Talleyrand (France, 18th–19th centuries).

A diplomat who served successive regimes (Louis XVI, Napoleon, the Restoration).

Manifestation of the model: a master of adaptation. He survived where others were sent to the guillotine. Successful, wealthy, influential — yet his memoirs betray a weariness born of the endless masks. A classic political Chameleon, searching for stability in an unstable world.

Niccolò Machiavelli (Italy, 15th–16th centuries).

Philosopher and statesman, author of the treatise The Prince.

Manifestation of the model: he understood the mechanics of how power adapts better than anyone. His teaching that the end justifies the means is, in essence, a manual for the Chameleon. Yet in his personal life he clung to republican ideals, which reveals the inner conflict between adaptation and conviction.

Oscar Wilde (Great Britain, 19th century) — his late period.

A writer who played the role of the aesthete in society while suffering inwardly from being misunderstood.

Manifestation of the model: in society he was the Chameleon, crafting an image. In prison and exile he began to write sincerely (De Profundis), burning his own mask. An example of moving from the Chameleon to a deeper self through suffering.

Henry Kissinger (USA, 20th century).

Secretary of State, famous for his diplomatic flexibility.

Manifestation of the model: the ability to find a common language with any leader in the world. His success and influence were at their peak, but critics pointed to the absence of a clear moral position — typical of the Chameleon model, where adaptation matters more than truth.

Joseph Fouché (France, 18th–19th centuries).

Statesman and chief of police, who survived the French Revolution, the Directory, the Consulate, the Empire, and the Restoration.

Manifestation of the model: they called him “the Chameleon of the Revolution.” He served any master, keeping his power and influence intact. His ability to bend with any political wind was legendary, yet in his contemporaries’ memoirs he remained a tragic figure, because no one ever knew his true face.

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

Depending on the context, a person may face an existential crisis.

When adaptation stops bringing satisfaction, the crisis begins.

The depressive exit: The person realizes: “I am successful, but I am not me.” The Chameleon withdraws into themselves and starts searching for meaning in psychology, yoga, art. This is a search for themselves. If they find a Mission, they move on to the Phoenix model. If not, they remain forever searching.

The aggressive exit: The person is angry at the world because success did not bring happiness. They may become a cynical manipulator, using their adaptability solely for power. This is being stuck in the Chameleon without moving toward the Phoenix. And yet, if they find a value higher than the crowd’s opinion, they become a Leader.

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “CHAMELEON”

Two years had passed since Maria took the job as a cleaner. She was no longer a Beggar: she had learned to work, learned to survive. But she had also learned something else — to be accommodating.

First she simply adjusted to the schedule, then to her boss’s moods, then to the clients’ tastes. She became a mirror: if a client wanted to talk, she talked; if he wanted silence, she kept quiet. She was perfect, and they noticed her. Six months later she was offered the position of administrator in another office — a higher salary, better status.

Maria accepted and became a mirror again. With colleagues she was easygoing; with management, deferential — and they praised her. “You’re our best employee,” the director said. A year later another big company lured her away with a manager’s position. This was success — the very kind she had dreamed of back in the Lilith era.

Another six months passed. Maria sat in an office with a panoramic window, and the city lay at her feet. She had a good salary, friends, a man who said he loved her. In the morning she put on the suit the director liked; in the daytime she spoke the words the clients liked; in the evening she smiled the way her man liked. But in the evenings, on her way home, the same question sounded in the silence of her apartment:

“Who are you?”

She looked in the mirror, and a successful woman with empty eyes looked back. She remembered her past lives: the Lilith who demanded money; the Puttana who screamed; the Cinderella who endured; the Beggar who pleaded. Now she was the Chameleon — all of them at once, and none of them.

The inner dialogue started slowly, like a heavy wave: “I adapted. I survived. But I am not living. Did I come into this world alone, to leave it alone? No. That is a lie. That is a defense.”

That is the trap of the Chameleon model: a person adjusts to other people’s expectations for so long that one day he no longer recognizes his own face. There is success, but no one there who could live it.

She picked up a book from the desk — The Phoenix Era. Its pages were filled with her notes. She read about Miyamoto Musashi — about how the spirit matters, not the sword.

She did not know the answer to her question. But she knew that adaptation is a dead end: to be convenient is to be invisible. And she wanted to be visible, even if it hurt.

The next morning she came to the office, but something in her had already changed. When a colleague started gossiping, she didn’t join in and calmly said, “I’m not interested.” The colleague was surprised. When the boss offered her unpaid overtime, she replied, “No. I have plans.” The boss frowned. Just yesterday she would have volunteered to stay until midnight, if only to avoid seeing that displeased look.

Maria felt fear, and the Chameleon inside her panicked: “You’ll be fired! No one will love you!”

But beneath the fear was something else — solid and hot, like a coal under ashes.

At lunchtime she went up to the roof. The city roared below; she took a deep breath, and at that moment her phone vibrated in her pocket. It was Edgar — the one from the foundation. She used to ignore his calls, but this time she answered.

“Hello,” she said without a mask, in her own voice.

Edgar didn’t talk long: he was inviting her to come. Maria closed her eyes. The wind tossed her hair, and she remembered all her models, all her masks — Lilith, Puttana, Cinderella, the Chameleon. Now she saw that these had been steps — a staircase she had been climbing.

“I’ll come,” she said. “But not as an employee. As myself.”

She put the phone away. Below, the city hummed; above, the sky, and she understood: the Chameleon must die — not physically, but psychologically. The masks must burn, so that something real could rise from the ashes.

She didn’t know what lay ahead: therapy, the School, the path of the Master. But she knew one thing: she no longer wanted to change her color. She wanted to shine.

Maria turned to the door. Below, her job was waiting for her; ahead was Life. She took a step — not a step of adaptation, but a step of choice.

It was the first step toward the Phoenix.
