Chapter 2. The relationship model · “PUTTANA”

Rebellion against morality: The trap of brazen protest.

1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The model takes its name from the Italian word “Puttana,” which translates as “prostitute.” But in the context of our method, the concept goes far beyond the physical sale of the body. It is a psychological archetype of a personality that, through trauma and protest, rejects the norms of society.

“I know better than anyone,” “Don’t teach me,” “I can do anything,” “I do what I want,” “I’m great” — when thoughts like these flow through a person’s mind, the end result is that they stop developing new knowledge and skills and instead grab attention through actions that can shock society. What happens then in the story of their life? They try to feel loved through people who worship not their personality but their fame. Such people cannot tolerate criticism and are susceptible to flattery. Lacking any inner love or praise for themselves, they feel cheated in this double game and go looking for that feeling elsewhere. In the end, they trust no one and try to expose the lies of everyone around them. In their youth they are the center of attention, but unable to build sincere and lasting relationships, they usually become a lonely and sorrowful figure after middle age. And because the mindset underlying this behavior never changes, no matter how much they suffer from loneliness, they find the cause in the people who left them. This, in turn, lowers their trust in people, deepens the feeling of isolation, and prevents them from building their next relationship. Naturally, the models of relationships will be as varied as the human psyche is changeable. These cognitive styles of thinking have nothing to do with heredity, personality types, or fixed yardsticks. They are connected only with the adopted, internalized model of thinking expressed in behavior and speech. That is why even a child from a modest family, despite the social prohibitions instilled early on, may later come to defy them and display rebellious behavior. Alongside people who regret their way of life and suffer for it, there are plenty of others who seem to deliberately destroy their own reputation. To approach this state from a psychological angle, let’s recall those who openly sell their bodies. From their choice of clothing to the way they move and speak — every meta-signal carries the message: “You can use me.” First, let’s remember that a person begins to take shape as a personality within society from birth. Socialization plays a significant role in human thinking at every stage of life. At each stage, those they constantly communicate with — loved ones, family members, neighbors, relatives, coworkers — matter greatly in what kind of person they grow into and how their needs get met.

Here is a small example to make this concrete. If at some stage of life a person falls into the world of prostitution or begging, changes begin in their psychology and worldview at the level of beliefs. These shifts affect not only their attitude toward that occupation but their general views on people and values. And since they are human themselves, they involuntarily project these thoughts onto themselves. Watching injustice in society, as it were, they conclude: “People are beasts,” “People are selfish.” Gradually they apply this not only to those they have encountered, but announce it to strangers at the very first meeting. Sooner or later, having accepted this thought as truth, they begin noticing the same traits in their own behavior. And trying to be honest with themselves, they seem to admit: “You’re a beast too, you’re selfish too — accept yourself as you are, fear nothing, live as you are.” As a result, a thought born in the conflicts of their environment under the pressure of emotions grows into a life philosophy and shows itself in their dealings with people in every sphere. At the same time, by recognizing themselves as the epicenter of these negative qualities, they intensify their aggression toward themselves (auto-aggression, depression). This, in turn, leads to bad habits, impulsive actions, a tendency toward conflict, and inflicting bodily harm on themselves and others. Even one such thought steers their worldview toward aggressive convictions and breeds new ones — and in the end they reject the values of their environment, declaring them False Values, and begin to rebel against its moral foundations. With time, their relations with those around them grow strained, and their rebellious behavior extends to society’s prohibitions and taboos. Since society’s strictest prohibitions concern sexual relations, the behavior of such a person seems to push them into defying the taboo. Someone who rejects society’s rules and rebels against them declares that they recognize no prohibitions in the sexual sphere either. And so society judges them to be more available, more depraved. It is fitting to call such behavior “puttana.” However hard they struggle in this pit, they will find no way out. They will look at those who condemn them with hatred, seeing in them violators of their personal boundaries. In their micro-environment, internal norms connected with the “I” are already forming and being accepted — but they are unloved. Just picture it: a person is dissatisfied with themselves, avoids looking in the mirror, fears loneliness, and so is constantly among people. And at the same time they consider themselves superior to everyone. This unhealthy self-assurance keeps dragging them into arguments.

In Hindu mythology, Puttana was a demoness (Rakshasi) sent by the tyrant Kamsa to kill the infant Krishna. She took the form of a beautiful woman to deceive everyone around her, but her true essence was destructive. According to legend, she tried to poison Krishna with her milk, yet the divine power of the child drew the life out of her, forcing Puttana to reveal her true demonic form before she died. Encoded in this myth is the meaning of the model: the loss of spirituality and morality in favor of destruction, the attempt to hide inner pain behind a mask of defiance.

If in the “Lilith” model the priority was material provision, here the priority becomes a demonstration of total permissiveness. This is a relationship model in which the personality expresses itself through traumatic pain, protesting against all of society. It is a form where there is no shame, no disgrace — a state one could even call worse than prostitution, because what is sold here is not the body, but conscience and dignity, all for attention and self-affirmation.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

People in the “Puttana” state of mind often broadcast thoughts that become their shield against the world. Here are the three core beliefs characteristic of this model:

“I know better than anyone — don’t tell me how to live my life.” (A belief in one’s own superiority over society’s morality).

“I can do anything — I do what I want.” (A denial of consequences and boundaries).

“I don’t care what you think.” (A demonstrative indifference to criticism that conceals a dependence on attention).

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

What happens in a person’s life story when this model takes shape? Often it is a reaction to a deep trauma. Someone whose personality has been under pressure tries to reclaim a sense of control through shock. They begin to draw attention not with talent or kindness, but with actions that shake society.

In psychoanalytic terms, this is a rebellion of the Id against the Superego. The Superego is responsible for morality and values, and the bearer of the “Puttana” model deliberately mutes that voice. They become intolerant of criticism but greedy for flattery. In their youth, such people may be the center of attention, but after middle age, loneliness and depression often await them. They build no deep connections, because they distrust people, seeing liars in everyone.

The psychological mechanism: a person can fall into an environment where amoral behavior thrives (for example, a milieu tied to selling the body or to a marginal way of life). The psyche adapts: in order to survive, they begin to see the norms of that environment as “correct.” A cognitive distortion arises: “All people are cruel and selfish, so I will be the same, so I won’t be a victim.” This grows into a philosophy of life. They begin to see the same negative traits in themselves and accept them: “Be who you are — fear nothing.” And so aggression against oneself (auto-aggression) spills out as aggression against the world.

Distinguishing this from borderline disorder: it is important not to confuse this model with the clinical borderline personality disorder. Although the symptoms may overlap (emotional instability, conflicts), “Puttana” is a behavioral model, not necessarily a pathology. A person can be the leader of rebellious movements, drawing followers around them. History offers many examples of scientists, politicians, or leaders of social movements (for example, radical feminists, eco-activists, members of LGBTQ movements) displaying the traits of this model through defiance of society’s taboos. Even Sigmund Freud in his day was perceived as a rebel violating Victorian morality with his theories.

Literary and mythological context: recall the Hindu myth of Puttana. She tried to deceive the world with beauty, but carried poison inside. It is the same with the bearer of this model: outwardly they may look successful and free, but inside they carry destruction — for themselves and for those close to them. It is the tragedy of losing the “true Self” for the sake of a mask of protest.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Puttana” model — defiance of morality, shock tactics, protest through self-destruction.

Sigmund Freud (Austria, 19th–20th centuries).

In the Victorian era, his theories of sexuality and the unconscious were considered scandalous and unacceptable.

Manifestation of the model: the text of the method points to him directly as an example of a “Puttana” in the context of rebellious behavior. He threw down a challenge to the foundations of society and was ostracized by his colleagues for it. His persistence in advancing his ideas despite public condemnation demonstrates a refusal to accept society’s restrictions for the sake of his own truth.

Marquis de Sade (France, 18th century).

An aristocrat who made amorality and cruelty the philosophy of his life and work.

Manifestation of the model: an absolute denial of shame and morality. His life and writings became a symbol of total permissiveness. He embodied a model in which pleasure is placed above any human norms — which corresponds to the definition of a “Puttana” as a state without shame or disgrace.

Sid Vicious (United Kingdom, 20th century).

Bass guitarist of the punk band “Sex Pistols,” an icon of the punk movement.

Manifestation of the model: shock tactics, drugs, violence, arrests. His life was a demonstrative protest against consumer society and its norms. He did not hide his fall — on the contrary, he took pride in it, which led to an early tragic death. It is a textbook example of protest through self-destruction.

Lady Gaga (USA, 21st century) — early stage of her career.

At the start of her path she used shocking costumes (the meat dress) and provocative performances.

Manifestation of the model: using shock tactics to grab attention. Though she later transformed, her early image was built on breaking taboos and shocking the public — typical of the “Puttana” model in its search for attention through scandal.

Jean Genet (France, 20th century).

A writer, thief, and vagrant who turned his criminal biography into literature.

Manifestation of the model: Genet openly celebrated crime, betrayal, and homosexuality at a time when all of these were taboo. He did not try to hide his “dark” side — on the contrary, he made it his brand and his weapon against bourgeois morality. His life shows a journey from the bottom of society to recognition through the demonstrative violation of every possible norm, which fits the “Puttana” archetype perfectly: the one who turns shame into pride.

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

Transformation and Crisis: If the “Puttana” achieves public recognition, her self-criticism weakens and her actions grow harsher. But when the fans turn away, when the fuel of rebellion runs dry, the collapse comes. The inability to vent the accumulated rage turns into aggression against the self. A person may find themselves facing loneliness or switch to a counterattack.

The depressive exit: The person is left alone. The fans drift away. Psychosomatic pains appear (headaches, heart pain). Then comes the realization that those they trusted have betrayed them. “People are unreliable — I was too naive.” This is the bifurcation point. The person may begin to hide their true intentions, becoming cautious and two-faced — a transition to the Chameleon model. Or they may withdraw into themselves, devaluing all connections — a transition to the Hippie model.

The aggressive exit: The person decides to punish those who turned away. They use the same methods of manipulation, but now with the aim of doing harm. If they have public influence, they may use it to hound their opponents. This only deepens their isolation. In the worst case, it leads to crime or total social downfall. Yet if the person can recognize their own wound, they may transform the energy of rebellion into defending others (a transition to Teresa) or into the search for a genuine connection (a transition to the Chameleon).

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “PUTTANA”

The mirror reflected the face of a stranger: the former softness had vanished, and in its place froze the resolve of a predator. Maria ran her finger along the cold glass, as if tracing the borders of new territory she intended to conquer. In her eyes, the last fire of hope was burning out — burning out so it could become fuel for war.

“So, I’ve hit rock bottom,” flashed through her mind, and the thought was sharp, almost crystalline. “I was used like disposable dishware and then tossed out. I was appraised as a line item and ruthlessly cut.”

Inside her there was no boiling resentment, the kind a victim feels, but a cold, calculating fury — the kind available only to someone with nothing left to lose. The status she had guarded so carefully had turned to dust. But can’t dust become poison?

“If I’ve already lost everything, why should I guard what’s left of my decency? If I’m falling into the abyss, why not drag someone down with me? Society loves to talk about morals, about propriety, about what a woman ought to be. But it was that same society that pushed me to the edge. They wanted to see me as a beautiful toy in Rafael’s shop window? Fine. Now they’ll see the monster that smashes shop windows.”

This is how revolt is born from despair: a pain too great to bear — and a person turns it against the whole world and calls it freedom.

She turned away from the mirror. Shame was a luxury for those who had a future; all she had left was the right to revenge.

She launched her campaign in the very first month — and she began not with a shout, but with a click. The phone screen became her new altar, and she took every like as a sacrifice in the name of justice. Maria posted revealing photographs, but her main weapon was words: she wrote about how women are bought, how the “costs” of mistresses get cut, real lives reduced to accounting ledgers. She never named names outright, but the hints were as clear and sharp as a scalpel’s blade.

“You want to know the price?” she thought, typing out the text that thousands would see a second later. “I’ll show you the receipt.”

The posts racked up millions of views and spread across the internet like a virus. People wrote: “Shameful.” “Hero.” “Whore.” She read everything and laughed, and her laughter rang like shattered glass. Every comment, every condemnation was just more fuel for her bonfire: she burned her shame to keep warm, somehow, in the cold of her loneliness.

In the second month, anger became her way of life — her breathing and her sleep. Or rather, she had all but stopped sleeping, because sleep reminded her of weakness. At night she wandered through clubs where strobe flashes tore distorted faces out of the darkness; she changed partners without remembering names, without even studying their features. They were not people to her but instruments — disposable, like syringes, through which the illusion of power was injected into her veins. In the morning she returned to an empty apartment where half-drunk bottles stood on the table, and, without undressing, sat down with her phone to read what had been written about her overnight.

“You want my body?” she thought, looking at the next man with a collector’s gaze. “Take it. But know this: you’re just an instrument in my hands. I use you to forget him. I use you to feel that I still exist.”

She felt powerful — intoxicatingly, dangerously powerful. Not long ago she had been a thing in Rafael’s hands, an object of consumption; now she herself had become poison in a glass that no one dared to drain to the bottom.

“I’m standing on the edge of an abyss,” she whispered to herself in the mornings, when the illusions washed away along with her makeup. “But it’s from the edge that you see everything. Especially those who stand at the top and are deathly afraid of falling.”

The climax came in the third month, at a restaurant where the air smelled of expensive wine and hypocrisy. There she saw him — Rafael, with his family. She recognized him instantly, from the way he held his wineglass: confidently, possessively, the same way he had once held her life in his hands. They were laughing, eating, living their proper lives as if she — Maria — had never existed.

Inside her, everything came to a boil, like lava in the throat of a volcano.

“Why do you get to live in peace?” flashed through her mind. “Why did you cut my allowance while you drink wine that costs a thousand? Why is your conscience asleep while mine is screaming?”

Maria walked up to their table without thinking, letting instinct lead her. Her step was steady, even though she was walking across a minefield — one she had laid herself.

Rafael went pale, his face contorted into a grimace of fear. His wife looked at Maria with a mute question, pity written all over it.

“Leave,” he hissed, as if swatting away an annoying fly.

Maria burst out laughing — loud, hysterical, and the laughter sounded like a snapped string.

She overturned the glass, and the wine ran down the white tablecloth, spreading into a red stain that looked like blood.

Security silently took her by the arms and led her out through the hall, where every conversation had fallen silent at once, onto the street — and there she was left alone. The wind whipped at the thin fabric of her dress and crept under her skin, but this cold seemed warmer to her than the icy void that had opened up inside.

She had expected triumph: she felt that victory would arrive any moment and that the taste of revenge would be sweet as nectar. But inside there was only silence — hollow and empty, like a hall after a play, when the audience has gone home and only the stage sets remain.

“And now what?” she thought, and the thought fell into the void without an echo.

She looked at her hands. They were trembling — not from the cold, but from emptiness. And then she understood: she had turned herself into a weapon, and a weapon doesn’t feel; it only destroys. And when the enemies are gone, it begins to rust.

It was already the fourth month. One morning began not with sunbeams but with a hum in her head, like a malfunctioning mechanism. The phone blinked on the floor like a wounded bird, and messages surfaced on the screen one after another: “You’re destroying yourself.” “Look at yourself.” “You’re finished.”

Maria sat down and surveyed the apartment. Empty bottles stood like tombstones of her former life, and the shards of a broken vase glittered on the floor, reminding her that wholeness cannot be pieced back together.

She walked to the mirror. A woman stared back at her, with dark circles under her eyes like shadows of exhaustion, and smeared lipstick that looked like traces of a struggle. Maria studied this face for a long time, and then asked it something out loud — quietly, hoarsely, not knowing herself what answer she was waiting for.

The silence answered her.

And something clicked inside — not loudly, but softly, like a switch cutting off the power in a house.

“I was taking revenge on him, but I was destroying myself. I was shouting to the world that I was free, but the world saw only my pain. I thought I would burn him, and I burned myself instead.”

She understood that the rebellion had not filled the void, and the scream had not drowned out the pain. Yes, she had freed herself from Rafael, but not from his judgment, and not from the judgment of society: she was still playing by their rules — she had merely changed pieces in this endless game. And therein lies the trap of the Puttana: rebellion against someone else’s opinion still remains a dependence on it.

Maria packed her things — one bag, only the essentials, as if preparing not for a journey but for an escape from her own skin. She left the keys on the table. The apartment stayed empty — as empty as her soul.

It was raining outside. Drops drummed on the asphalt, washing away the city’s dust, but she didn’t open her umbrella. Let it soak her. Let it wash away everything — everything, to the very end.

She boarded a train — without a ticket, without a plan, without a goal, simply to keep moving, so that the distance between her and her past could be measured in kilometers.

“Where are you headed?” the conductor asked.

Maria didn’t answer: she was looking out the window, where the city was dissolving into the fog like sugar in water.

The train pulled away and picked up speed. The wheels clattered, counting off the seconds of a new life. She closed her eyes, and for the first time in four months she felt no anger — only exhaustion, deep, bone-deep.

And somewhere in that exhaustion something new was being born. Not freedom yet, no. But a silence — a real one: not the kind that filled Rafael’s apartment, thick with waiting for a phone call, but the kind that comes from within. The silence after a storm.

She didn’t know where she was going or what awaited her, but she knew one thing: the rebellion was over. Something else was beginning.

The train gathered speed. The city vanished, and only the road remained ahead. And the silence.
