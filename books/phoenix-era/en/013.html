We have completed the first stage of our investigation. Before us now lies a panorama of human shadows: ten relationship models, which we have examined in detail. This is a classification of the neurotic behavioral patterns that psychological practice encounters. These are ten survival strategies a person chooses in an attempt to fill an inner emptiness with external surrogates.

Lilith sought security in gold, convincing herself that love could be bought. But the currency of feelings doesn’t convert into a bank account. Puttana turned pain into rebellion, shouting at the world to drown out her own fear. But aggression builds no bridges; it only burns them down. Hippie fled responsibility into the illusion of freedom, but freedom without purpose becomes a wind that doesn’t know where to blow. Geisha sought admiration on the stage of life, trading sincerity for applause. But the ovations fade, leaving behind only the exhaustion of the performance. Mistress clung to her partner like a life raft, mistaking dependence for love. But no one can breathe someone else’s air forever. Courtesan turned relationships into a hunt, collecting victories. But trophies can’t embrace, and the cold of collected achievements seeps into the soul. Teresa sacrificed herself, buying the right to exist through suffering. Infanta was afraid to take a step without permission—an adult body with the soul of a child. But life doesn’t wait for someone to give the command. Cinderella carried other people’s burdens, believing it was her duty, and dissolved into caring for others. But someone else’s load doesn’t straighten your back; it only breaks the spine of the personality. Beggar demanded his due from the world, feeling like a victim of circumstance. But the world owes us nothing, and demanding love as a tax is doomed to refusal.

If we follow the logic of depth psychology, all these models share one thing: they are built on false needs. Instead of communication and love, the person sought security, attention, power, or an excuse. It is an attempt to quench thirst with salt water: the more you drink, the stronger the thirst. Neurotic models are defense mechanisms that arose as a reaction to trauma but hardened into stereotypes. They do not create wholeness; they merely preserve the pain.

When a person experiences the collapse of each of these masks, when they understand that neither gold, nor rebellion, nor freedom, nor sacrifice has given them the answer, they find themselves at a crossroads. They no longer want to be Lilith or Cinderella. But they do not yet know who they really are. To survive in society, to avoid being rejected, they begin trying on the masks of those around them. They become convenient for everyone. They reflect other people’s desires like a mirror.

The transition from neurotic relationships to the model of healthy relationships.

Beneath all these neurotic behavioral models lies one highly significant factor. Every disharmonious relationship model is built to satisfy a person’s unhealthy needs. By unhealthy needs we mean needs that lie beyond direct communication and love. To make this clearer, let’s look at an example. From birth, a person has a set of physiological and psychological needs. Food, water, air, sleep, the elimination of bodily waste—all of these are physiological needs. If they are not satisfied promptly, a person’s life is in danger; they may die. Security, communication and love, respect and self-actualization are psychological needs. If psychological needs are not met in time, there is no real threat to a person’s health. And yet, for a person to feel happy, those needs must be met. It is precisely in the process of satisfying psychological needs that a person, developing, takes shape as a personality and earns a worthy place in society, under their own name, with their own abilities and talent. The psychological need for communication and love mentioned here is the only healthy need on which relationships can be built. In other words, every person needs to feel not alone, to feel a loving sense of belonging to a community, and to be loved. This need, which unites people, builds society, develops humanism, awakens feelings of mercy and empathy, and elevates a person spiritually. If, however, people prefer to satisfy not this need through their relationships but some other one, or if they pursue personal and social goals tied to no needs at all, this gives rise to unhealthy needs. For example, a person builds a relationship in order to feel secure. This is an attempt to satisfy one need by means of another. To draw an analogy with physiological needs, it is as if a person, wanting to quench their thirst, decides to get more sleep. Or tries to stifle hunger with deep breathing. Compared to physiological needs, this, of course, sounds absurd. After all, everyone knows: if a physiological need is not satisfied in time, no matter how much a person deceives themselves, death is inevitable. As for psychological needs, when a person tries to satisfy one of them by means of another, they deceive both themselves and those around them. Naturally, if unhealthy needs are glaringly obvious in a person’s relationships, this is no accident. It is evidence that the person has not yet matured as a personality and is not yet ready to satisfy their own needs independently. And it is under the pressure of whichever need they feel weakest in that they experience inner tension, building relationships to satisfy it. In this way, unhealthy needs influence a person’s thinking, their spirituality, their values, their life principles, their convictions and, of course, their relationships with other people.

Such unhealthy emotional bonds are often built not on genuine, sincere love but, in most cases, on the satisfaction of a sexual need. That is why relationships born of these neurotic behavioral models are unstable and inauthentic. These relationship models resemble a theatrical performance. The person believes in it, deceives everyone around them, and in the end complains that they cannot be happy.

Is there, then, a relationship model born of healthy needs?

A person who has tried out all the false relationship models, who has recognized the lie at their foundation, has found the courage to renounce them, and after that has decided to build their life only on sincere love flowing from the heart is like the Phoenix: they have turned their past to ashes with the fire of truth and created themselves anew. This is the Mature Personality, one who stands out from others at first glance: captivating with a radiant smile, disarming with sincerity, moving with courage toward their own dream. Is it possible for a person to be a Phoenix from birth? If you meet such a person, know this: it is a miracle; this child is holy.

Before we plunge into the depths of the Phoenix’s relationship model, let’s examine the path that leads to healthy relationships. How does it happen that a person, once their thinking has changed and they take both themselves and all the needs of life into account, stops making mistakes in dealing with people?

First of all, the book The Phoenix Era, as a study guide, makes it possible to identify the particular mindset that keeps a person within one relationship model or another for a long time. If a person wants to be free of unpleasant feelings, they will begin to observe themselves. On this path they will be guided no longer solely by their personality type but above all by the thoughts that hinder them. Knowing what kind of mindset reproduces the same reactions and situations over and over, they will begin to investigate the causes that gave rise to this mindset, in order to change it. On the path of personal growth, in order to free themselves from the same thoughts that influence their consciousness, they will begin to take action, deciding what and how to change in the future. Only then can a person protect themselves against the recurring scenarios of their own life.

The cause of psycho-emotional complexes also lies in the way the mindset takes shape in the course of a person’s personal development. The convictions that lay the foundation of a personality’s identity contain, above all, information about what a person is by nature. When a person realizes: “Since by this age I have taken shape as a bearer of such persistent feelings, the cause lies in my thinking. Why do I invariably find myself faced with the same attitude toward myself? What makes me repeat these actions again and again?” then, with this awareness, they are able to move into The Phoenix Era faster.

Knowing this path, every person is capable of working on themselves tirelessly at the stages of personal growth. This is the sure path to a healthy, sincere life filled with love. No other assumptions offer this possibility. In the human world, it is not enough merely to know about various pathologies. Since many schools offer no information about the mindset that holds a person’s behavior within its former frames, it is hard to quickly find the way to a solution. In The Phoenix Era this is possible.

How does the transitional period unfold? If a person in a stable behavioral model encounters unpleasant circumstances and unconsciously looks for ways out, their choice turns toward the search. Like someone drowning at sea, flailing with all their strength, they begin to look for a path to salvation. They are ready to seize the first thing that comes to hand. In such circumstances, a strange, unfamiliar process may be born in them against their will. The behavioral model of a person who has followed different ideas all their life and run into new difficulties every time can be given one more name: the Chameleon relationship model.

Thus the Chameleon is born.

This is not the finale. This is the antechamber. This is the last frontier before the true “Self” rises from the ashes. After the Chameleon comes the territory of healthy models, where a person lives not for survival but for meaning. But to reach it, one must pass through adaptation.
