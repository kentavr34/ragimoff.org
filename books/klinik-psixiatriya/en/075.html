1. Definition and nosology

Disorders due to use of sedatives, hypnotics or anxiolytics (ICD-11: 6C44; DSM-5-TR: F13.20 Sedative, Hypnotic, or Anxiolytic Use Disorder) — impaired control over use of benzodiazepines, Z-drugs (zolpidem, zopiclone), barbiturates, and other anxiolytics. Often begins with a medical prescription in clinical practice; ‘iatrogenic dependence’ is common.

2. History

  1. 1960s — clinical introduction of benzodiazepines (chlordiazepoxide 1960, diazepam 1963).
  2. 1980s — recognition of the dependence potential of long-term use.
  3. NICE and APA — short-term use recommendation (2–4 weeks).

3. Epidemiology

  • Long-term benzodiazepine use in the elderly: 10–20%; problem identification is low.
  • Sex: higher prescription rates in women.
  • Comorbidity: alcohol and opioid use (dangerous combination — overdose risk).

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

  • Iatrogenesis — long-term prescriptions.
  • Adaptation to the GABA-A receptor system — tolerance and discontinuation.
  • Self-medication pattern in patients with anxiety, sleep disturbance.

5. Clinical features

  • Dependence syndrome — loss of control, tolerance, withdrawal.
  • Intoxication — sedation, ataxia, dysarthria, disinhibition, anterograde amnesia.
  • Overdose — life-threatening respiratory depression with opioids.
  • Withdrawal — may be life-threatening (like alcohol): tremor, anxiety, tachycardia, hypertension; severe cases: seizures and DT-like syndrome.

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR — 11 criteria (AUD structure). Unlike DSM-5-TR, which uses a single severity scale, ICD-11 uses three separate categories: episode of harmful use, harmful pattern of use, and dependence. ICD-11 duration requirement: harmful pattern of use — at least 12 months if use is episodic, at least 1 month if continuous; dependence — at least 12 months, or at least 3 months if use is continuous (daily or almost daily).

6.2 Differences between sources

  • NICE — benzodiazepine prescription 2–4 weeks; long-term use should be reassessed.
  • Beers Criteria — avoid benzodiazepines in the elderly.

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  • Clinical interview + prescription history.
  • Toxicology screening.
  • Comorbid alcohol and opioid use (especially overdose risk).
  • Clinical examination — withdrawal signs.

6.4 Differential diagnosis

Condition Distinguishing feature
Anxiety disorder Comorbid; substance use is symptomatic.
Alcohol use Frequently comorbid.
Opioid use Comorbid; overdose risk high.

7. Examination and assessment

  1. Toxicology screening.
  2. Vital signs (discontinuation)

8. Treatment

  • Gradual discontinuation (taper) is the gold standard: conversion to equivalent dose of long-acting benzodiazepine (diazepam), then gradual reduction, usually by 10–25% every 2 weeks (Pottie 2018); with long-term use more slowly, by 5–10% every 2–4 weeks; total duration often lasts months. Rapid discontinuation can be life-threatening.
  • Hospitalization — severe withdrawal or comorbid substance use.
  • Psychosocial intervention — CBT for anxiety or sleep disorder; this reduces the need for pharmacotherapy.
  • Comorbid disorders treatment (anxiety, depression, sleep).
  • Prescription management — at the primary healthcare level.

8.1 Treatment methods

  1. Ashton Manual Taper — Conversion to diazepam equivalent, then gradual reduction; family support.
  2. CBT for Anxiety and Sleep — Alternative to benzodiazepine use; CBT-I for sleep.
  3. Toxicological Monitoring — During the maintenance period.

8.2 Differences between sources

  1. NICE, BNF — taper protocols.
  2. Ashton Manual — practical clinical guide by Heather Ashton (C.H. Ashton); 2002 edition, revised 2011.

9. Prognosis

  • Significant improvement with gradual taper and CBT.
  • In older adults, discontinuation is both medically and psychologically difficult.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “Benzodiazepines can be taken safely for life”

Evidence: long-term use — tolerance, dependence, cognitive impairment, fall risk (in the elderly). NICE — short-term use.

Myth 2: “Rapid discontinuation is safe”

Evidence: Rapid discontinuation carries a risk of seizures and death; gradual taper is critical.

Myth 3: “Z-drugs (zolpidem) are not addictive”

Evidence: Z-drugs also cause dependence; AAFP and NICE recommend short-term use.

Myth 4: “Benzodiazepine + opioid is safe”

Evidence: FDA boxed warning — this combination significantly increases the risk of respiratory depression and death.

Myth 5: “Detoxification is sufficient”

Evidence: After discontinuation, alternative treatment (CBT, anxiety management) is required.
