1. Definition and nosology

Disorders due to use of stimulants including amphetamines, methamphetamine or methcathinone (ICD-11: 6C46; DSM-5-TR: F15.20 Stimulant Use Disorder) — disorders developing from use of amphetamine, methamphetamine, methcathinone and other stimulants. Coded separately: cocaine (6C45), synthetic cathinones including mephedrone (6C47), caffeine (6C48), MDMA or related drugs including MDA (6C4C).

2. History

  1. Amphetamine was first synthesized by Edeleanu L. (1887); widespread use in World War II.
  2. 1950s–1960s — prescription-drug epidemic.
  3. Methamphetamine epidemic since the 1990s.
  4. “Bath salts” (synthetic cathinones) — epidemic of 2010+. “Krokodil” (desomorphine) is an opioid and is coded separately in ICD-11 (6C43).

3. Epidemiology

  • Global users: amphetamine 30 million+ (UNODC).
  • Sex: higher in males.
  • Comorbidity: psychosis, MDD, anxiety, cardiovascular, skin lesions (self-excoriation, ‘meth mouth’).

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

  • Heritability 40–50%.
  • Neurobiological — release of dopamine and norepinephrine and blockade of their reuptake; severe neurotoxicity (especially methamphetamine — to dopamine neurons).

5. Clinical features

  • Intoxication — euphoria, hypervigilance, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia; in severe cases: paranoid psychosis, agitation, myocardial infarction, stroke, seizures, rhabdomyolysis.
  • Withdrawal — “crash” — depressive mood, anhedonia, fatigue, hypersomnia, increased appetite, intense cravings; high suicide risk.
  • Chronic use — psychosis (paranoid delusions, hallucinations), cognitive deficit, “meth mouth” (tooth decay), skin picking.
  • MDMA (6C4C) — hyponatremia, hyperthermia, serotonin syndrome, neurotoxicity.

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR 11 criteria (AUD structure). Unlike DSM-5-TR, which uses a single severity scale, ICD-11 uses three separate categories: episode of harmful use, harmful pattern of use, and dependence. ICD-11 duration requirement: harmful pattern of use — at least 12 months if use is episodic, at least 1 month if continuous; dependence — at least 12 months, or at least 3 months if use is continuous (daily or almost daily).

6.2 Differences between sources

  • SAMHSA TIP 33 — stimulant use.
  • NIDA — methamphetamine research reports.

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  • Clinical interview.
  • Toxicology.
  • ECG, troponin.
  • Comorbidity (psychosis, MDD, cardiovascular).

6.4 Differential diagnosis

Condition Distinguishing feature
Cocaine (6C45) Toxicology.
Manic episode Substance temporal relationship.
Schizophrenia No remission after substance elimination.
Hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma Endocrine.

7. Examination and assessment

  1. Toxicology.
  2. ECG, troponin, CK (rhabdomyolysis).
  3. Dental and skin examination.
  4. C-SSRS.

8. Treatment

  • Psychosocial — first-line:
    1. Contingency management;
    2. CBT;
    3. MI;
    4. Matrix Model — manualized comprehensive program for amphetamine dependence.
  • Pharmacotherapy — No specific FDA-approved medication exists; off-label: mirtazapine, naltrexone + bupropion (Trivedi M.H. NEJM 2021 — ADAPT-2 trial combination for methamphetamine — modest effect).
  • Acute intoxication — benzodiazepine for agitation; antipsychotic in psychosis.
  • Comorbid psychosis — parallel treatment.

8.1 Treatment methods

  • Matrix Model — 16-week structured program — CBT + family + 12-step + single physician. Evidence base for amphetamine/methamphetamine is moderate.
  • Contingency Management — Reward for negative test.
  • Naltrexone + Bupropion (ADAPT-2) — Primary RCT evidence in methamphetamine addiction; off-label.

8.2 Differences between sources

  1. SAMHSA TIP 33.
  2. NIDA.
  3. Trivedi M.H. et al. NEJM 2021 — initial evidence base for naltrexone + bupropion in methamphetamine use disorder.

9. Prognosis

  • With multimodal approach, remission 30–50%.
  • Comorbid psychosis indicates a poor prognosis.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “Methamphetamine is safer than ‘Spice’ or other ‘designer drugs’”

Evidence: Methamphetamine induces high neurotoxicity, psychosis, and mortality.

Myth 2: “MDMA is safe as a ‘pure’ substance”

Evidence: MDMA — hyperthermia, hyponatremia, serotonin syndrome; ‘pure’ substance is typically adulterated (synthetic cathinones, fentanyl).

Myth 3: “Detoxification is sufficient for stimulant dependence”

Evidence: Contingency management and CBT-based treatment are effective; detox alone leads to relapse >90%.

Myth 4: “Antidepressants cure stimulant addiction”

Evidence: a single antidepressant is ineffective; ADAPT-2 provides preliminary evidence for the combination.

Myth 5: “Stimulant users are ‘productive’ and do not require intervention”

Evidence: Short-term performance boost illusion; long-term cognitive, psychiatric, cardiac complications.
