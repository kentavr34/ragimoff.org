1. Definition and nosology

ICD-11: 6E40 Psychological or behavioural factors affecting disorders or diseases classified elsewhere; DSM-5-TR: F54 Psychological Factors Affecting Other Medical Conditions — psychological factors interfering with somatic disease course (depression, anxiety, medication adherence, stress, beliefs about illness).

Distinct from factitious disorder (Munchausen syndrome; 6D50) and bodily distress disorder (6C20) — here there is a confirmed medical illness, but psychological factors influence the course.

2. History

  1. Alexander F. (1950) — conceptualization of ‘psychosomatic medicine’.
  2. Engel G.L. (1977) — ‘biopsychosocial model’.
  3. Levenson J.L. (2018) — APA Textbook of Psychosomatic Medicine.

3. Epidemiology

  • Universal — psychological factors influence all chronic somatic diseases.
  • Depression in cardiovascular disease — 20–40%; diabetes — 25%; cancer — 15–25%.
  • Consultation-liaison psychiatry services are widely used.

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

Biopsychosocial interaction — biological predisposition + psychological factors + social context.

5. Clinical features

  • Comorbid depression/anxiety affects illness (e.g., increased mortality after myocardial infarction).
  • Medication non-adherence (compliance/adherence).
  • False beliefs about illness.
  • Stress worsens illness course.
  • Behavioral factors (smoking, alcohol, diet, physical inactivity).

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria

A. Confirmed medical illness present.

B. Psychological or behavioral factors interfere with illness course:

  • Reduces treatment response;
  • Increases health risk;
  • The pathophysiology of the illness is aggravated;
  • Psychological factors interfere with medical treatment.

C. Psychological or behavioral factors are not better explained by another mental disorder (e.g., panic disorder, major depressive disorder, PTSD).

6.2 Differences between sources

  1. DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 — same.
  2. Consultation-liaison psychiatry model.

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  • Evaluation of medical illness.
  • Identification of psychological factors — depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), medication adherence (Morisky).
  • Behavioral factors — smoking, alcohol, diet, physical activity.
  • Screening for comorbid psychiatric disorder.

6.4 Differential diagnosis

Condition Distinguishing feature
Bodily distress disorder (6C20) Somatic complaint dominant.
Factitious (6D50) Conscious lie.
MDD comorbid with medical illness Affective symptoms meet independent criteria — separate diagnosis.

7. Examination and assessment

  1. PHQ-9, GAD-7, Morisky.
  2. Illness-specific scales (DDS — Diabetes Distress Scale, BDI-II in cardiology).

8. Treatment

  • An integrated multidisciplinary approach — somatic specialist + psychiatrist + psychologist.
  • Illness-specific psychosocial intervention:
    1. Cardiac CBT (post-MI depression);
    2. Diabetes self-management education (DSME);
    3. Oncology distress monitoring;
    4. Chronic pain CBT and ACT.
  • Antidepressant — disease-specific side effect profile:
    • Cardiac — sertraline, escitalopram;
    • Oncology — mirtazapine (sleep, appetite benefit);
    • Chronic pain — duloxetine, amitriptyline.
  • Motivational interviewing — behavior change (smoking, physical activity).

8.1 Treatment methods

  • Consultation-Liaison Psychiatry — integrated service in medical-surgical departments.
  • Cardiac CBT — Post-MI depression management.
  • Diabetes Self-Management Education (DSME) — Diabetes self-management training.
  • CBT and ACT for chronic pain — Williams Cochrane 2020.

8.2 Differences between sources

  1. NICE CG91 — CBT for depression in cardiac patients.
  2. Levenson J.L. APA Textbook of Psychosomatic Medicine 2018.

9. Prognosis

Integrative approach improves both somatic and psychological outcomes.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “The illness is entirely psychological in origin”

Evidence: “Biopsychosocial” model — psychological factors influence but do not alone cause somatic illness.

Myth 2: “The psychiatrist is ‘running away’ from the illness”

Evidence: integrated approach — somatic and psychiatric intervention parallel.

Myth 3: “Alternative medicine (yoga, mindfulness) cures somatic illness”

Evidence: Complementary interventions may be adjunctive and do not replace standard somatic treatment (oncologic, cardiac).

Myth 4: “Telling the patient ‘you got sick because of stress’ is therapeutic”

Evidence: This approach is accusatory and undermines the therapeutic relationship.
