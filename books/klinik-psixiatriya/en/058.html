1. Definition and nosology

Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder (ICD-11: 6B66; DSM-5-TR: F48.1) — a dissociative disorder characterized by persistent or recurrent depersonalization (feeling detached from oneself, disconnected from body and thoughts) and/or derealization (surroundings appearing unreal, like a ‘foggy film’). Reality testing is preserved (the patient knows the experience is unreal).

2. History

  • Krishaber M. (1873) — first description.
  • Dugas L. (1898) — term ‘dépersonnalisation’.
  • DSM-III (1980) — Separate diagnosis.
  • DSM-5 (2013) — depersonalization and derealization as a combined category.

3. Epidemiology

  • Lifetime prevalence: 1–2% in clinical form; subclinical experiences at least once in 50% of the population.
  • Sex: approximately equal.
  • Onset: adolescence and early adulthood (mean 16 years).
  • Comorbidity: MDD, panic, GAD, PTSD, BPD.

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

  • Trauma and stress trigger (especially cannabis, hallucinogen use).
  • Neurobiological — impaired prefrontal-limbic regulation; sensory processing anomalies (Simeon D. reviews).
  • Comorbid anxiety is common, and panic attacks are frequent.

5. Clinical features

  • Depersonalization: Sense of observing oneself from outside, feeling like a robot, emotional flattening, feeling that “my body is not mine”.
  • Derealization: Environment appears “faded”, “like a faded film” or “artificial”; distance perception change.
  • Reality testing preserved — patient knows the experience is unreal.
  • Significant distress and functional impairment.
  • Typical course — continuous or recurring episodes.

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria

A. Persistent or recurrent experiences of depersonalization and/or derealization.

B. Reality testing preserved.

C. Significant distress or functional impairment.

D. Exclusion of substance (cannabis, hallucinogen) or medical condition (seizure, brain injury).

E. Not better explained by another mental disorder (transient during panic attack; in the context of PTSD; schizophrenia).

6.2 Differences between sources

  • DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 — same criteria.

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  1. Clinical interview.
  2. CDS (Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale, Sierra M., Berrios G.E.) — 29 items.
  3. Medical and neurological examination (excluding seizures and brain injury).
  4. Toxicology screening.
  5. Comorbidity.

6.4 Differential diagnosis

Condition Distinguishing feature
Panic attack Short-term in the context of an attack.
PTSD (6B40) Against a background of trauma; intrusive symptoms.
DID (6B64) Distinct identities.
Seizure (epileptic) EEG; postictal state.
Schizophrenia Reality testing impaired.
Substance-induced (cannabis, LSD) Toxicology.

7. Examination and assessment

  • CDS is the gold standard.
  • EEG if seizure is to be ruled out.
  • Toxicology.

8. Treatment

  1. CBT — adapted for depersonalization; cognitive restructuring + exposure. Hunter E.C. et al. Behav Res Ther 2005.
  2. SSRI — effective for comorbid depression and anxiety; evidence base for the disorder itself is limited.
  3. Lamotrigine adjunct — effective in some studies.
  4. Against a background of trauma — trauma-focused approach.
  5. Abstinence from cannabis and hallucinogen use is critical.

8.1 Treatment methods

  1. CBT for depersonalization — Hunter (Hunter E.C.), Sierra (Sierra M.) — Cognitive restructuring, exposure, grounding techniques.
  2. Cambridge Depersonalisation Scale (CDS) — 29 items.
  3. Grounding techniques — 5-4-3-2-1 (5 visible objects, 4 sounds, etc.); sensory attention.

8.2 Differences between sources

  • Sierra M. reviews — CBT and adjunctive SSRIs.
  • There is no specific FDA-approved medication.

9. Prognosis

  • Chronic course, but significant improvement with CBT.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “Depersonalization is a sign of psychosis”

Evidence: Reality testing preserved; distinct from psychotic disorder; antipsychotics are ineffective.

Myth 2: “Depersonalization is transient and does not require treatment”

Evidence: The clinical form is characterized by a chronic course and functional impairment; intervention recommended.

Myth 3: “Cannabis improves depersonalization”

Evidence: Cannabis is a trigger and perpetuating factor; abstinence is critical.

Myth 4: “Benzodiazepines are the main treatment”

Evidence: Benzodiazepines provide short-term relief, but carry a risk of dependence and depersonalization enhancement.

Myth 5: “Only antidepressants are effective”

Evidence: CBT first-line; SSRIs for comorbid symptoms.
