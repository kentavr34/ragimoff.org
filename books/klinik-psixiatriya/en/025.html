1. Definition and nosology

Bipolar type II disorder (ICD-11: 6A61; DSM-5-TR: F31.81) — a form of bipolar disorder characterized by at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, but never a full manic episode. It is not a ‘milder’ form than Type I — patients spend more of their lifetime in depression; functional impairment and suicide risk are comparable to Type I.

2. History

  • Dunner D.L., Gershon E.S., Goodwin F.K. (1976) — proposal to differentiate bipolar disorder subtypes.
  • DSM-IV (1994) — Bipolar Type II as a separate diagnostic category formalized.
  • DSM-5 (2013) and ICD-11 (2019) — Type II retained, emphasizing that it is not a ‘mild form’.

3. Epidemiology

  • Lifetime prevalence: ~0.4–1.1% (Merikangas K.R. Arch Gen Psychiatry 2011 WMH).
  • Sex: more common in females (~1.5:1 — different from Type I).
  • Onset: mean 20–25 years; multiple depressive episodes usually precede a hypomanic episode.
  • Suicide risk comparable to Type I (lifetime ~5–7%); more often due to depressive episodes.
  • Comorbidity: anxiety 60%+, substance use 30–40%, borderline personality disorder.

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

  • Genetic basis overlaps with Type I — heritability 60–80%; GWAS loci overlap.
  • Genetic independence of the clinical phenotype from bipolar Type I is debated — some studies assess both types as the same spectrum.
  • Circadian rhythm disturbance and family history.

5. Clinical features

5.1 Hypomanic episode

  • Abnormally elevated or irritable mood + increased energy ≥ 4 days (≥ 7 in Type I).
  • Same sub-symptoms as manic episode (decreased need for sleep, distractibility, increase in goal-directed activity, etc.); ≥ 3 symptoms (≥ 4 if irritable mood).
  • Functional impairment should not be significant (i.e., hypomanic definition); no psychotic features; no hospitalization required. If the episode goes beyond these limits (significant impairment, psychotic features, or hospitalization), it is a manic episode and the diagnosis changes to Type I.

5.2 Major depressive episode

  • ≥ 5 symptoms for 2 weeks (including low mood and/or anhedonia).
  • Bipolar Type II depressive episodes often with atypical features — hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity.
  • Patients are symptomatic during 53.9% of follow-up weeks; depressive symptoms occupy 50.3% of weeks (hypomanic 1.3%, cycling/mixed 2.3%) — Judd L.L. et al. Arch Gen Psychiatry 2003, n=86, mean 13.4 years of follow-up; most symptoms are at subsyndromal level.

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria

A. At least one hypomanic episode.

B. At least one major depressive episode.

C. Never had a manic episode.

D. Hypomanic and depressive episodes are not better explained by schizoaffective disorder, schizophrenia, or another psychotic disorder.

E. Symptoms cause significant distress or functional impairment (primarily from depressive episode).

6.2 Differences between sources

  • DSM-5-TR / ICD-11: functional impairment in hypomanic episode should not be significant; antidepressant-induced hypomanic episode leads to bipolar Type II diagnosis (especially in persistent or recurrent cases).
  • Underdiagnosis problem: Angst J. et al. (BRIDGE study, Arch Gen Psychiatry 2011;68(8):791–798) — ~16% of patients presenting with a depressive episode meet DSM-IV criteria for bipolar disorder (types I and II) and a further 31% meet bipolar-spectrum criteria, but hypomanic episodes are perceived by the family as “good periods.”

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  1. Structured inquiry for hypomanic episodes in every depressive patient (SCID-5 mood module, MDQ, HCL-32).
  2. Information from family members — observation of hypomanic episodes is important.
  3. Clinical interview + YMRS, HAM-D / MADRS.
  4. Laboratory: thyroid, liver, kidney, toxicology, pregnancy test.
  5. Comorbidity and suicide risk assessment.

6.4 Differential diagnosis

Condition Distinguishing features
Bipolar Type I (6A60) ≥1 manic episode (functional impairment or hospitalization requirement).
Major Depressive Disorder (6A70/6A71) No hypomanic episode.
Cyclothymic (6A62) No full major depressive or hypomanic episodes.
BPD (6D11.5) Emotional lability hours; identity disturbance; chronic suicidal ideation.
ADHD Persistent; not episodic.
Hyperthyroidism TSH measurement.

7. Examination and assessment

  • SCID-5 mood module — the gold standard.
  • MDQ (Mood Disorder Questionnaire) and HCL-32 (Hypomania Checklist) — screening.
  • YMRS, HAM-D / MADRS, C-SSRS.
  • Pre-lithium: creatinine, TSH, calcium, ECG.

8. Treatment

8.1 General principles (NICE CG185 · CANMAT/ISBD 2018)

  1. Bipolar Type II depressive episode (most common presentation):
    • First-line — quetiapine (FDA approved); lithium, lamotrigine — second-line. Lurasidone’s FDA indication covers bipolar I depression only, not type II; CANMAT/ISBD 2018 likewise names quetiapine alone as first-line for the type II depressive episode.
    • Lamotrigine — first-line in maintenance and depressive prophylaxis (evidence mainly in Type I, also used in Type II).
    • Lithium — maintenance and suicide reduction.
  2. Hypomanic episode: Stop antidepressant; mood stabilizer (lithium, valproate) or atypical antipsychotic.
  3. Antidepressant use: formerly considered relatively safe in bipolar Type II, but in CANMAT 2018 monotherapy (sertraline, venlafaxine) is second-line, usually with a mood stabilizer (to reduce risk of manic switch and rapid cycling).
  4. Maintenance: Lamotrigine (when the depressive component is dominant) or quetiapine; lithium for suicide reduction effect.
  5. Psychosocial: CBT, IPSRT, family-focused therapy, psychoeducation.
  6. Refractory: clozapine, ECT.
  7. Pregnancy: valproate is contraindicated in pregnancy and may be used in women of childbearing potential only under the Pregnancy Prevention Programme; lamotrigine and quetiapine relatively safe.

8.2 Treatment methods

  1. Lamotrigine — Bipolar Type II effective in maintenance and prevention of depressive episodes; titration slow (risk of Stevens–Johnson syndrome) — 25 mg/day × 2 weeks, then 50 mg/day × 2 weeks, then gradually to 200 mg/day (doses halved with valproate). Calabrese J.R. et al. J Clin Psychiatry 2003 (type I study).
  2. Quetiapine — FDA approval for bipolar depressive episode; 300–600 mg/day; metabolic side effect monitoring.
  3. Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT; Frank E. et al.) — psychotherapy for bipolar disorder aimed at circadian rhythm regulation, treatment adherence, and relapse prevention.
  4. Hypomania Checklist (HCL-32) — Angst J — 32-item self-assessment for hypomanic episodes.
  5. Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ — Mood Disorder Questionnaire) — 13 questions; bipolar screening.

8.3 Differences between sources

  • CANMAT/ISBD 2018: Type II depressive episode first-line — quetiapine; lamotrigine in maintenance.
  • NICE CG185: Types I and II share the same main approach, but lamotrigine’s specific role emphasized in Type II.
  • Calabrese J.R. RCTs — lamotrigine efficacy in maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder (the main evidence comes from type I patients).

9. Prognosis

  • Episodic course; relapse significantly reduced with maintenance pharmacotherapy.
  • Type II is not ‘milder’ than Type I — high functional impairment and depressive burden.
  • Suicide risk is high.
  • Quetiapine and lamotrigine as the main maintenance agents.
  • Monitoring — depressive symptoms, hypomanic triggers, metabolic indicators.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “Type II bipolar is a ‘mild form’ requiring little treatment”

Evidence: Judd L.L. Arch Gen Psychiatry 2003 — Type II patients spend 50.3% of follow-up weeks with depressive symptoms (53.9% symptomatic overall); functional impairment and suicide risk comparable to Type I.

Myth 2: “Hypomanic episode is a ‘good period’, no treatment needed”

Evidence: hypomanic episode can involve risky behavior, impaired relationships, later productivity decline, and trigger a depressive episode; isolated episodes indicate need for maintenance pharmacotherapy.

Myth 3: “Antidepressant alone can be used in patients with Type II disorder”

Evidence: CANMAT 2018 — antidepressant only with mood stabilizer; risk of manic switch and rapid cycling.

Myth 4: “Bipolar Type II is only a form of borderline personality disorder”

Evidence: There is significant overlap and comorbidity, but these are distinct disorders; bipolar Type II is episodic, whereas BPD follows a continuous pattern.

Myth 5: “Lamotrigine cures a manic episode”

Evidence: Lamotrigine is effective in the depressive component and maintenance; ineffective for manic episodes — antipsychotic or valproate is required.

Myth 6: “St John’s Wort is safe in bipolar because it is a natural antidepressant”

Evidence: St John’s Wort can induce mania and decrease the levels of mood stabilizers (CYP induction); contraindicated in bipolar disorder.

Myth 7: “ECT is not needed in Type II”

Evidence: ECT is indicated for refractory depressive episodes or high suicidality risk; also effective in patients with Type II disorder.
