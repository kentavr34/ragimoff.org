Complex post traumatic stress disorder (cPTSD)

ICD-10F43.1Post-traumatic stress disorder
DSM-5-TRF43.10Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (no separate DSM-5-TR category)
Definition and nosologyHistoryEpidemiologyEtiology and pathogenesisClinical featuresDiagnosisExamination and assessmentTreatmentPrognosisMyths and misconceptionsSources

1. Definition and nosology

Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (cPTSD; ICD-11: 6B41) — a disorder that develops in response to prolonged or repeated trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible (prolonged abuse, childhood physical or sexual violence, war, torture, human trafficking). It includes the three symptom domains of PTSD (re-experiencing, avoidance, persistent sense of threat). To these is added “disturbance of self-organization” (DSO): affect regulation impairment, negative self-esteem, difficulty in relationships.

In ICD-11, added for the first time as an official diagnostic category; DSM-5-TR does not have a separate cPTSD category (the PTSD dissociative subtype qualifier covers some features).

2. History

  • Herman J.L. (1992) — “Complex PTSD” concept (book “Trauma and Recovery”); “Disorder of Extreme Stress Not Otherwise Specified” (DESNOS) — not included in DSM-IV.
  • ICD-11 (2019) — official code 6B41; based on evidence separating it from PTSD (reviews by Cloitre M. et al.).

3. Epidemiology

  • Prevalence: 1–8% in the general population; higher in trauma-exposed (reviews by Brewin C.R. et al.).
  • Particularly high in patients with a history of childhood abuse.
  • Comorbidity: MDD, GAD, dissociative disorders, BPD (clinical overlap), substance use, eating disorders.

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

  • Trauma type — prolonged, repeated, perpetrated by others (interpersonal), difficult escape.
  • Trauma occurring in childhood — broad impact on personality development.
  • Neurobiological — similar to PTSD but with more widespread HPA dysregulation, dissociation, and impaired affect regulation.

5. Clinical features

PTSD consists of three primary domains.

  • Re-experiencing (intrusions).
  • Avoidance.
  • Persistent sense of threat.

Self-organization disturbance (DSO) — core component of cPTSD

  • Affect Dysregulation — emotional variability, intense reactions, emotional flattening.
  • Negative self-esteem — persistent feelings of shame, guilt, worthlessness.
  • Difficulty in relationships — avoidance of close relationships, persistently impaired attachment.

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria (ICD-11)

A. Exposure to long-term or repeated trauma.

B. PTSD has three main domains.

C. Self-organization disturbance (DSO), with three domains:

  1. Affect Dysregulation;
  2. Negative self-esteem;
  3. Difficulty in relationships.

D. Significant functional impairment.

6.2 Differences between sources

  • ICD-11 — separate category; DSO requires additional criteria.
  • DSM-5-TR — no separate cPTSD category; PTSD ‘with dissociative symptoms’ specifier covers some characteristics.
  • ISTSS expert consensus (Cloitre 2011) — recommends phased approach.

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  1. Clinical interview + trauma history (long-term, repetitive).
  2. ITQ (International Trauma Questionnaire — Cloitre M.) — designed for differentiating PTSD and cPTSD.
  3. Comorbidity (especially BPD, dissociative disorders).
  4. Assessment of suicide and self-harm risk.

6.4 Differential diagnosis

Condition Distinguishing feature
PTSD (6B40) No DSO component.
BPD (6D11.5) Clinical overlap significant; in BPD, impulsivity, identity disturbance, and chronic suicidality are dominant; in cPTSD, symptoms develop in response to trauma.
Dissociative disorders (6B6x) Dissociation predominates.
MDD (6A70/6A71) Affective symptoms; no trauma and no DSO.

7. Examination and assessment

  • ITQ (International Trauma Questionnaire) — validated instrument for distinguishing PTSD from complex PTSD.
  • PCL-5 + DSO additional scales.
  • BPD screening (McLean Screening Instrument for BPD, MSI-BPD).
  • Suicide and self-harm (C-SSRS).

8. Treatment

8.1 General principles (ISTSS 2019)

  1. Phase-based approach:
    • Phase 1 — stabilization — Safety, emotional regulation skills, coping strategies, psychoeducation.
    • Phase 2 — processing of traumatic memories — Evidence-based methods for PTSD (PE, CPT, EMDR), but usually require longer duration and are more persistent.
    • Phase 3 — reintegration — Restoration of relationships, improvement of quality of life.
  2. Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR — Cloitre M.) — a phase 1 intervention before processing traumatic memories.
  3. Trauma-focused CBT and EMDR — adapted format.
  4. DBT-PTSD (Bohus M.) — in comorbid BPD cases.
  5. SSRI adjunct — comorbid depression and anxiety.
  6. Duration of treatment is long (1–2 years or more).
  7. Clinician continuity and therapeutic alliance are critically important.

8.2 Treatment methods

  1. Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR) — Cloitre M. — Phase 1 — emotional regulation and interpersonal skill development; then processing traumatic memories via Narrative Storytelling (NST). 16–24 sessions.
  2. Dialectical Behavior Therapy — PTSD (DBT-PTSD) — Bohus (Bohus M.), Linehan (Linehan M.) — DBT principles + trauma-focused components; especially in comorbid BPD cases.
  3. Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET) — Schauer M., Neuner F., Elbert T. — The patient narrates life history in sequence, traumatic episodes are processed; effectiveness in refugees and torture victims.
  4. EMDR, CPT, PE — adapted — Within a longer, phased approach.
  5. International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) — Cloitre M. — Specific validated tool for distinguishing PTSD and cPTSD.

8.3 Differences between sources

  • ISTSS 2019 Complex PTSD Guidelines — phased approach.
  • Cloitre M. et al. reviews — STAIR + Narrative Story Telling effective.
  • Bohus M. DBT-PTSD — RCT evidence base is developing.

9. Prognosis

  • Chronic course; but significant improvement with phased approach.
  • Comorbid BPD, dissociative disorders, substance use poor prognosis.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “Complex PTSD is just a new name for borderline personality disorder”

Evidence: Clinical overlap significant, but ICD-11 cPTSD is trauma-based; BPD is a separate personality disorder. ITQ and BPD scales differentiate; most cPTSD patients do not meet BPD criteria.

Myth 2: “After the trauma is ‘resolved,’ the patient normalizes”

Evidence: Long-term trauma affects personality development; ‘closure’ is insufficient; phased approach and long-term treatment are required.

Myth 3: “Standard PTSD treatment (PE, CPT) is equally effective for cPTSD”

Evidence: ISTSS 2019 — phased approach in cPTSD; the patient must first be stabilized before trauma processing (phase 1); direct exposure may pose a risk of re-traumatization.

Myth 4: “Only an antidepressant is sufficient for complex PTSD”

Evidence: Psychotherapy is first-line; pharmacotherapy in an adjunctive role.

Myth 5: “The patient must be made to talk about the trauma immediately”

Evidence: Trauma processing without Phase 1 stabilization can be harmful; trauma-informed care principles.
