Chapter 6. The Relationship Model “Courtesan”

The art of possession: The trap of endless victory.

1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The name of the model comes from the term “Courtesan” (Italian: cortigiana), which first appeared in the mid-15th century in the courts and salons of Renaissance Rome. Historically, this is the image of a nobleman’s beloved, a woman of high birth who led a worldly, society life. Unlike an ordinary prostitute, the courtesan was not obliged to sell her body in order to survive; what she sought in relationships was the game, the competition, and the confirmation of her own exceptionalness.

If you follow the logic of the historical context, this is the aristocracy of vice. In Greek mythology, echoes of this archetype can be seen in the figure of Aphrodite, the goddess of love, beauty, and passion, who changed lovers at the call of desire rather than necessity. In the terminology of the method, the “Courtesan” is about prioritizing the quantity of victories over the quality of the bond, where love becomes a battlefield.

The roots of the image reach back to the Renaissance. High status allowed courtesans to dictate the terms. They were educated, elegant, and dangerous. In the context of our method, the “Courtesan” is not necessarily a woman; it is a psychotype for whom sexual attractiveness is the main weapon for confirming one’s own significance. It is a model in which relationships are perceived as entertainment and competition, not as a union.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

People in the state of mind called the “Courtesan” often broadcast thoughts that become their philosophy of seduction. Here are the three core beliefs characteristic of this model:

“A person’s life can hold many lovers.” (Denial of the exclusivity of the bond.)

“He won’t resist my charm.” (Confidence in the power of manipulation.)

“Love is a game where I always win.” (Perceiving relationships as a competition.)

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

What happens in a person’s life story when this model takes shape? Often it is the consequence of an inferiority complex hidden behind a mask of confidence. The person does not believe in their own value as an individual, so they confirm it through the number of hearts conquered. Unlike the “Geisha,” who gathers a horde of admirers (admiration for talent), the “Courtesan” leads a horde of lovers (admiration for flesh and charm).

In psychoanalytic terms, what happens here is a compensation for sexual insecurity. If a person has doubts about their sexual potential, they strive to prove the opposite through the number of partners. This is not a dependence on money, as with the “Lilith” or the “Mistress.” The Courtesan is often financially independent. She may spend her own money on lovers, give them gifts. What matters to her is not the financial support but the fact of possession. She carries no obligations toward her partners, considering the gifts merely a confirmation of their love, not payment for services.

Psychological mechanism: The person realizes they lack outstanding intellectual or creative abilities to attract attention any other way. So appearance, clothes, makeup, and emotional behavior are brought into play. In adolescence, such a person learns to flirt better than their peers, to play with feelings. This becomes their core model. They hide their dark sides, striving for quick results. This often shows up as the desire to get a partner into bed on the very first date, a mark of success.

A modern analogue of the courtesans can be seen in pick-up artist communities. They create communities where they discuss how to make a person fall in love with them quickly. This is propaganda for the need to confirm one’s sexual identity through the number of victories. They present themselves as sexual gurus. At the root of it, though, lies doubt about their own potential. They try to show quality through quantity, yet in reality they cannot build a sincere connection.

Distinction from other models: it is important not to confuse this model with “narcissistic personality disorder.” Narcissism is a stable personality type, while the “Courtesan” is a model of thinking that can be changed. The Courtesan is not afraid of responsibility the way the “Hippie” is; she is afraid of exposure. She avoids official marriage, saying: “A stamp in a passport cools the feelings.” It is insurance against intimacy, where she might be seen for who she really is.

Literary context: Let us recall the novel Dangerous Liaisons. Its heroes, the Marquise de Merteuil and the Vicomte de Valmont, compete in the art of seduction. This is the Courtesan model in its purest form: emotions as weapons, love as a duel. Or Guy de Maupassant’s novel Bel Ami, where the protagonist Georges Duroy uses women to advance his career, remaining free of obligations. This is the male version of the Courtesan model.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Courtesan” model: many lovers, love as a game, confirming status through possession.

Giacomo Casanova (Italy, 18th century).

A Venetian adventurer whose name became a byword.

Manifestation of the model: The classic male archetype of the Courtesan. He kept detailed diaries of his conquests, viewing love as an adventure and a way to confirm his own vitality. His relationships were vivid but short-lived, because the main goal was conquest, not holding on.

Catherine II the Great (Russia, 18th century).

An empress famous for her favorites.

Manifestation of the model: Despite her status, her personal relationships were often built on the principle of choosing a partner for pleasure and companionship, with the option of replacement. She affirmed her power and femininity by keeping favorites, while remaining an independent ruler, which matches the freedom of the Courtesan.

Mae West (USA, 20th century).

An actress and sex symbol famous for her witty one-liners about love.

Manifestation of the model: She openly declared: “When I’m good, I’m very good, but when I’m bad, I’m better.” She managed men, replaced them, used her sexuality as an instrument of power, while remaining financially independent and free of obligations to her partners.

Virginia Oldoini, Countess of Castiglione (Italy/France, 19th century).

An aristocrat known for her beauty and her influence over Napoleon III.

Manifestation of the model: She used her looks and charm for political and personal ends, collecting admirers and lovers among the highest nobility of Europe. Her life was a performance in which she was the leading actress, and the men were spectators and players in her game.

Don Juan (Literary archetype).

The legendary seducer, hero of countless works (Molière, Byron, Pushkin).

Manifestation of the model: The symbol of the male Courtesan. For him, the number of conquests matters more than the quality of feeling. He cannot stop at one woman, because every new victory is proof that he exists. His tragedy is that he looks for in love what love cannot give: eternal confirmation of his worth.

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

A person may run into guilt or rejection.

When the illusion of victory collapses (the partner learns the truth or walks away), a crisis sets in.

Crisis of the model: The crisis arrives when the Courtesan meets the person of the highest status she can possibly find. Winning him over becomes her life’s mission. She brings all her talent, all her cunning to bear, to make him fall in love with her. But once the goal is achieved, the worst part begins. The lies used for the conquest (the fake feelings, the promises) start to surface. The partner realizes he was not loved sincerely, but as a trophy. He feels deceived and sinks into depression. The Courtesan realizes that instead of her greatest victory she has scored her greatest defeat. She made a person unhappy with her own methods. Guilt over someone else’s suffering can drive her into a deep depression.

The depressive exit: The person realizes: “I hurt the one I wanted to conquer.” The Courtesan withdraws into herself and starts searching for sincerity. She may begin trying on different masks, attempting to figure out who she really is (the transition to the Chameleon model). Or she decides to atone for her guilt by devoting herself to one person, sacrificing her freedom (the transition to the Cinderella model).

The aggressive exit: The person is angry at the partner for “not understanding her game.” “Why did they reject my love?” The Courtesan grows intolerant and cynical and begins to take revenge, seducing others out of spite. This deepens her isolation and leads to the Puttana model (a revolt against morality and feelings).

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “COURTESAN”

Two years had passed since that morning with the envelope. Maria was living in another city, under another name and with a different haircut, and remembered her former self as one remembers a poor relation. She had carried one lesson out of the country house: whoever waits, loses. So she would be the one waited for.

She worked at an agency that matched wealthy men with companions for dinners and trips, and very soon she was doing the choosing herself. There were three men in her life at once. One took her to restaurants and liked her to argue with him, another sent flowers and expected her to listen in silence, and the third simply admired her, and with him she laughed a low, throaty laugh she had never had in her life. Not once did she mix up her lines.

When the first of them confessed that he had never met a woman like her, Maria gave a smile calibrated in the mirror:

“That’s because you haven’t seen anyone yet except me.”

He laughed and kissed her hand. She noted to herself that the line worked and saved it for the other two.

“I’m not attached to anyone,” she thought, coming home at dawn and kicking off her shoes in the entryway. “I take attention, gifts, proof that I’m worth something, and I pay for it with a lovely evening. Nobody is deceived. It’s all fair.”

In her phone she kept notes on each one: what he liked, what he feared, what never to bring up with him. She kept them neatly, the way she had once handled other people’s presentations, and she was proud of the order. She and a friend from the agency discussed the men the way people discuss horses before a race and laughed until they cried, and at such moments it seemed to Maria that she had finally become someone nobody would ever mark down.

Toward the end of the second year the men began to want more. One started talking about her being with him only; another suggested moving in together. The word “only” sent a chill through the pit of her stomach: she remembered the dark living room and the headlights on the drive too well. She would vanish without explanation and find a replacement within a week. The ones who were hurt got no reply. The one who sent flowers wrote her a long, bewildered letter, and she read it aloud to her friend, with expression.

With each new face, though, it grew emptier inside. She felt desired all the time and alive almost never, and in the mornings, with her makeup washed off, it took her a long while to remember what her face looked like when nobody was looking at it.

And then Timur appeared. At the agency his name was spoken in an undertone: a man of a standing she had never yet seen up close. He came to a charity evening alone, hardly drank, and didn’t look at the women. Winning him became a task for her, and then something like an illness. For three weeks she studied him before letting him catch sight of her: she learned what books he gave as gifts, what music he couldn’t stand, which partners he was on bad terms with. She learned that his mother had died in March and that he wouldn’t hear of pity.

A line appeared in her notes: “Mother: March. No pity. Happen to be nearby.”

On the anniversary she “happened” to be on the same waterfront where he was walking alone, and she didn’t utter one word of sympathy. She simply walked beside him and kept silent for exactly as long as needed. Three months later he was at her feet, though he would have put it differently himself.

“I trust you,” he said one evening, looking her straight in the eye. “I haven’t said that to many people.”

For an instant Maria was at a loss. Passion, jealousy, gifts were things she was used to, and this word came from some other life, one where people drink tea by a stove and ask no questions. For a second she wanted to tell him everything, starting with the bracelet, and see whether he would stay. The second passed.

“I trust you too,” she said, and her voice didn’t waver.

That evening she was as pleased with herself as a craftsman is with a cleanly done job. She texted her friend, “Grand prize claimed,” and added a laughing face.

Timur found out everything a month later. The one who sent flowers proved more patient than she had thought: he tracked down her friend from the agency, and the friend, nursing some grievance of her own, handed over their chats, screenshots of the notes, photos with the two others. It all reached Timur at once.

The confrontation took place at her apartment. Timur stood by the door, coat still on, the printouts in his lowered hand. He didn’t shout; he was waiting to hear what she would say.

Maria had already opened her mouth to explain that this was the revenge of a wounded man, a forgery, that she would tell him right now how it had really been. The words were ready, and she almost believed them herself. Then she looked into his face and understood that she couldn’t lie, and what stopped her wasn’t at all that he wouldn’t believe her. He would have believed her; that was the whole trouble.

“The part about my mother,” he said at last, and turned over the top sheet. “Was that on the list too? The waterfront?”

She said nothing.

“Maria,” he said. He had learned her real name from those same papers and was saying it for the first time. “And here I thought I’d gotten lucky.”

He laid the printouts on the little cabinet by the door, neatly, corner to corner, and left.

The apartment still smelled of his cologne. Yesterday it had been the smell of victory; today it turned her stomach. Maria sat down on the entryway floor and wrapped her arms around her knees. The voice that for two years had explained to her without a hitch why everything was fair was silent this time.

The phone on the cabinet glowed: ten missed calls from new admirers, a question from her friend about how it had gone. Maria looked at the screen and saw Timur’s face as he turned over the sheet. She had won from him everything she had set out to win and had done to him what Rafael once did to her, except that Rafael at least hadn’t pretended. Such is the Courtesan’s crisis discussed in this chapter: the greatest victory turns out to be a defeat.

That night she didn’t pick up a single call. She lay on the cold parquet and had no wish to justify herself or to be liked; she wished only that he hadn’t left with that look on his face.

A week later her friend told her that Timur had canceled all his meetings and gone away without telling anyone where. Maria wrote to him: “Forgive me.” The message was never delivered, and for many days she kept opening it to look at the gray check mark.

One thing she now knew for certain: she would never cause pain again. She would do only good, so much of it that it would outweigh that waterfront.
