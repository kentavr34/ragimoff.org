Chapter 11. The Relationship Model “Chameleon”

1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The model takes its name from a living creature: the chameleon. This reptile can change the color of its skin to match its surroundings, becoming invisible or, on the contrary, strikingly bright whenever it pays to do so. In the context of our method, the “Chameleon” is a person who has mastered the art of adaptation but lost their own color.

From the standpoint of evolutionary psychology, this is the highest form of social conformity. It is a transitional stage. To reach the level of the Phoenix, a person inevitably passes through the stage of the Chameleon. It is the moment when someone, having sampled every neurotic model, realizes that not one of them delivers wholeness. They begin trying each on for size, becoming convenient for everyone, but a stranger to themselves.

The roots of the image lie in politics and diplomacy. In the terminology of the method, the “Chameleon” means placing external success above inner sincerity. It is a model in which a person speaks the language of their interlocutor, and forgets their own.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

People in the “Chameleon” state of mind often broadcast thoughts that conceal their inner emptiness. Here are the three core beliefs typical of this model:

“With every person, you have to speak their language.” (The belief that sincerity is less effective than adaptation.)

“I have everything, and yet something is missing.” (A sense of existential emptiness against the backdrop of outward success.)

“A person comes into this world alone and leaves it alone.” (Justifying inner loneliness with a philosophy of fatalism.)

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

The path through chaos toward adaptation. A person often begins their journey with infantile dependence (the Infanta), then tries to buy safety (Lilith, the Mistress). They rebel against the system (the Puttana, the Hippie), try to conquer the world with talent (the Geisha, the Courtesan), or sacrifice themselves for others (Teresa, Cinderella). Sometimes they fall to the very bottom, demanding help (the Beggar).

If we follow the logic of the source, all of these models are neurotic. They are built on false needs. When each one comes crashing down, they find themselves searching. They start looking for a way out. And the first solution they find is adaptation. They understand: to survive, you must be convenient. In this way they come one step closer to the Chameleon.

Psychological mechanism: The Chameleon has a broad outlook. They know that different peoples hold different values, and they accept it. “Everyone has their own opinion, and everyone deserves respect” is their favorite phrase. They avoid conflict, because they read another person’s mood masterfully. In any company, they are a welcome guest. They get invited to events; they succeed in their career, especially in politics or diplomacy.

In transactional analysis terms, this is a block on one’s own Self.

The Chameleon does not know what they themselves want. They confuse their own desires with the desires of those around them. On the outside they look understanding; on the inside they suffer from loneliness. They ask themselves: “I don’t know what I want. Why do I live?”

Loneliness in a crowd. This is the key marker of the model. A person may be surrounded by friends, family, and colleagues, and still feel like a stranger. “I have everything: work, family, friends. And yet something is missing, as if there’s some unknown need.” That is the Chameleon’s typical complaint. Unlike other models, where a person suffers from a lack of resources or love, the Chameleon suffers from a lack of self.

The transition to the Phoenix: The Moment of Truth. How does a Chameleon become a Phoenix? For that, an inner upheaval is needed. A person must understand that adaptation is not the way. They must find their Mission. To illustrate, let’s take Miyamoto Musashi. The great Japanese samurai of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, a master of the sword. He won every duel, and came to understand that strength does not lie in the sword. Legend has it that once, unarmed, fending off attackers with an oar, he realized: mastery lives within, not in the instrument. He burned his old knowledge and founded a new school. So it is with the Chameleon. When they find a value that matters more than the crowd’s opinion, they burn their masks. They stop adjusting, and start leading.

The Phoenix is the final destination. The Phoenix is a mature personality. This is not a myth; it is a psychological reality. A person who has burned their neurotic models in the fire of awareness and been reborn in sincerity. They do not manipulate; they love. Their need is not safety or success, but connection and love for the sake of the process itself. This is the level where a person cares for others not for the sake of approval (as Teresa does), but because it is their nature. Like the Phoenix bird in the legend of Noah’s Ark, which asked for no food so as not to trouble Noah, and for that was granted eternal life. It is a symbol of mercy and independence.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Chameleon” model: adaptability, success, but a search for an inner mission.

Charles Maurice de Talleyrand (France, 18th–19th centuries).

A diplomat who served successive regimes (Louis XVI, Napoleon, the Restoration).

Manifestation of the model: a master of adaptation. He survived where others were sent to the guillotine. Successful, wealthy, influential, yet his memoirs betray a weariness born of the endless masks. A classic political Chameleon, searching for stability in an unstable world.

Niccolò Machiavelli (Italy, 15th–16th centuries).

Philosopher and statesman, author of the treatise The Prince.

Manifestation of the model: he understood the mechanics of how power adapts better than anyone. His teaching that the end justifies the means is, in essence, a manual for the Chameleon. Yet in his personal life he clung to republican ideals, which reveals the inner conflict between adaptation and conviction.

Oscar Wilde (Great Britain, 19th century), his late period.

A writer who played the role of the aesthete in society while suffering inwardly from being misunderstood.

Manifestation of the model: in society he was the Chameleon, crafting an image. In prison and exile he began to write sincerely (De Profundis), burning his own mask. An example of moving from the Chameleon to a deeper self through suffering.

Henry Kissinger (USA, 20th century).

Secretary of State, famous for his diplomatic flexibility.

Manifestation of the model: the ability to find a common language with any leader in the world. His success and influence were at their peak, but critics pointed to the absence of a clear moral position, typical of the Chameleon model, where adaptation matters more than truth.

Joseph Fouché (France, 18th–19th centuries).

Statesman and chief of police, who survived the French Revolution, the Directory, the Consulate, the Empire, and the Restoration.

Manifestation of the model: they called him “the Chameleon of the Revolution.” He served any master, keeping his power and influence intact. His ability to bend with any political wind was legendary, yet in his contemporaries’ memoirs he remained a tragic figure, because no one ever knew his true face.

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

Depending on the context, a person may face an existential crisis.

When adaptation stops bringing satisfaction, the crisis begins.

The depressive exit: The person realizes: “I am successful, but I am not me.” The Chameleon withdraws into themselves and starts searching for meaning in psychology, yoga, art. This is a search for themselves. If they find a Mission, they move on to the Phoenix model. If not, they remain forever searching.

The aggressive exit: The person is angry at the world because success did not bring happiness. They may become a cynical manipulator, using their adaptability solely for power. This is being stuck in the Chameleon without moving toward the Phoenix. And yet, if they find a value higher than the crowd’s opinion, they become a Leader.

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “CHAMELEON”

Two years had passed since Maria took the cleaning job. She had unlearned asking and learned to work, yet there was something else she had learned even better. After the pond, at her bathroom mirror, she had given herself her word that nobody would ever be afraid of her again, and she kept it with the diligence of someone who has once seen herself through a stranger’s eyes.

At first she adapted to the schedule, then to the shift supervisor’s moods, then to the habits of those who stayed late in their offices: with the one who wanted to talk she talked, and around the one who wanted quiet she moved without a sound. Six months later she was hired as an administrator at one of the offices on the ninth floor. “You’re our best employee,” the director would say. A year after that a large company lured her away as a manager.

Now she had an office with a floor-to-ceiling window, and the city that had once whispered to her about money and status lay below her all the way to the harbor. She had the salary she had dreamed of at twenty-four, she had friends, she had a man who said he loved her. She lied to none of them; with each one she was simply whoever suited that person. At the director’s jokes she laughed half a second before he finished them. With the cleaning women in the elevator she talked prices, and they counted her as one of their own. With her man she rooted for his team and knew the lineup better than he did, though soccer bored her to tears. She had paid Nina back in full, and Nina had said, “You’ve become so light.” Maria couldn’t tell whether it was a compliment.

“You have to speak to each person in his own language,” she explained to herself. “That isn’t pretending, it’s respect. Everybody is happy, and nobody is afraid of me.”

She decided to celebrate her fortieth birthday in style and invited everyone at once. That was a mistake. Gathered in one room were the director, her yoga friends, pals from the old office, and her man with two of his fellow fans. Maria stood in the middle of the living room, glass in hand, and couldn’t begin her toast: any sentence suited some and gave her away to others, and she didn’t know which voice to speak in or which laugh to laugh. The guests waited, and she was silent for so long that somebody made a crack about her age. She picked up the joke, and the evening rolled on.

Edgar called that evening. He called every year on that day, and every year she looked at his name until the screen went dark.

That night, after seeing off her guests, she stopped in front of the entryway mirror.

She asked aloud, “Who are you?”

Looking out of the mirror was a well-groomed woman with a pleasant face ready for any company. “I have everything,” Maria thought. “So why this feeling of having been robbed? They say a person comes alone and leaves alone. Maybe that’s just how it works.”

This is the trap of the Chameleon: a person adapts to other people’s expectations for so long that she stops recognizing her own face.

She bought the book a week later at an airport, the way she had bought every book in recent years: a client had said at dinner that he had read nothing better all year, and she needed to keep up the conversation. The cover read The Phoenix Era. At the back was a test, a hundred statements. “Answer honestly. There are no right or wrong answers here.” On the third statement Maria caught herself calculating which answer would give a better result. She was alone, in a window seat, and not a living soul would ever see these pages, yet she was choosing answers to make the book like her. Maria laughed so hard that the man in the next seat turned to look, and she started over.

She finished it at home, reading until morning, pencil in hand. In someone else’s book she recognized her own life: Rafael’s bracelet, the lamp in the studio, the headlights on the drive, the loaf in a stranger’s bag. For fifteen years she had believed that each time she was starting over. In fact she had been walking from room to room of a single house, and in each one looking for something to pay for love with: money, scandal, success, obedience, kindness, convenience.

The way out was described at the end, in three words: sincerity, love, mercy. In the book they were called pillars, and at first Maria skimmed them the way you skim the wishes on a greeting card. Then she went back and saw an order in them. They were three steps, and they could only be taken one after another. Without sincerity, without open feeling, there is no love, because you can love only someone you can see, and for fifteen years nobody had seen her; she had made sure of that. Without love there is no mercy. Mercy, of all things, her life had never lacked: she had mopped floors in a shelter, ladled out soup, fed Anton. And every time, behind her kindness stood an unspoken bill, and when the bill went unpaid she went to collect, as she had from Nina and her mother. Mercy without love and sincerity ends badly, and always the same way: in abuse of trust, in deception, in fraud. She had managed to be both the one who gives in order to buy and the one who is given to out of fear.

In the margin she wrote: “Sincerity, then love, then mercy. In that order only.” Below it she added, “What do I want?” and sat for a long time over the empty line, as she once had over a menu.

So there was no point in searching for a twelfth role, better than the earlier ones, nor in playing the sincere woman now. She had to stop hiding and start with herself, because there was no one else to start with: to tell the truth from the very beginning and not when there is nothing left to lose; not to be afraid that someone close would become necessary to her; not to be afraid to say “this is mine” and “I don’t want it this way.” By the time she closed the book the streetcars were already running outside, and she was scared, the way you are before jumping into cold water.

The first day came out clumsy. To a coworker who started in on the usual gossip she said, “I’m not interested,” and it sounded harsher than she intended, with an echo of the old anger. She had to catch up with him in the hallway and explain that it wasn’t about him, and halfway through she caught herself already playing a new role, the direct and honest woman, and fell silent. To the director, who suggested she work the weekend “as a friend,” unpaid, she answered:

“No. I have plans.”

The director frowned. Only a week earlier she would have offered to stay herself. “They’ll fire you,” the familiar voice thrashed inside her. “They’ll stop loving you.” Her hands were ice cold, yet beneath the fear lay something solid and hot, like a coal under ash.

That evening she told her man that she didn’t like soccer. He laughed, then realized she wasn’t joking.

He asked, “What else?”

“I don’t know. I’ve only just started sorting it out. Maybe a lot of things.”

He was silent for a long time and then said he needed to think. She didn’t try to persuade him, though persuading was something she knew how to do.

The next day at lunchtime she went up to the roof. Her phone buzzed in her pocket: Edgar. She looked at the name, and this time she answered.

“Hello,” she said in her own voice, one she herself hadn’t heard in a long time.

“Well, what do you know,” said Edgar. “Four years you didn’t pick up. It’s me again; I figured I’d try one more time. Listen, there’s a group that meets at the foundation on Saturdays now. They play a game based on a book. Come. And we’re short of hands, as always.”

About the book in her bag she told him nothing.

“I’ll come. But not as an extra pair of hands. As me.”

What lay ahead she didn’t know: therapy, the School, the path of the Master were so far only words from the last pages. She put the phone away and walked toward the stairs.
