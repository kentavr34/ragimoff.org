Chapter 4. The Relationship Model “Geisha”

The art of self-expression: The trap of public admiration.

1. NAME AND MEANING

Transcription and Etymology: The name of the model comes from the Japanese word “Geisha” (芸者), where “gei” means art and “sha” means person. Literally: “a person of art.” In Japanese culture, this is the image of a woman who entertains guests with her talent yet remains untouchable.

If we follow the logic of the historical context, this is not simply a profession; it is a philosophy of service through excellence. Unlike a prostitute, a Geisha sells not her body, but emotion, atmosphere, the illusion of perfection. In Greek mythology, echoes of this archetype can be seen in the figures of the Muses, goddesses who inspired creators but rarely became their wives.

The roots of the image go back to the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Historically, the first geishas were men, entertainers of the samurai (hōkan). Later, women replaced them. The key figure was Kikuya, a former courtesan who had won her freedom. She established a rule: a Geisha may enchant, but she is not obligated to belong physically. This divided them into “white” geisha (art only) and korobi geisha (the “falling” ones, who permitted intimacy). In the context of our methodology, “Geisha” is the priority of public recognition and talent over personal attachment.

2. SLOGAN AND BELIEFS

People in the “Geisha” state of mind often broadcast thoughts that become their manifesto. Here are the three core beliefs characteristic of this model:

“My talent demands sacrifices, and love is the first of them.” (A justification of loneliness in the name of success.)

“The admiration of the crowd matters more than the love of one.” (Public recognition takes priority over intimacy.)

“Reputation is the one thing you cannot afford to lose.” (Fear of criticism and of losing one’s image.)

3. INFORMATION BLOCK: HISTORY, LITERATURE, AND PSYCHOLOGY

What happens in a person’s life story when this model takes shape? Often it is compensation for an inferiority complex by reaching the very top. The person decides that their worth must be proven to the public. They begin attracting attention not through availability (as Puttana does), but through unavailability and mastery.

In psychoanalytic terms, what happens here is the sublimation of libido into creativity. If the Id demands pleasure, the Geisha transforms that demand into applause. She is ready to sacrifice personal happiness for the stage. In their youth, such people may seem inspired, but in maturity they often face loneliness surrounded by admirers. They do not build deep bonds, because they fear that someone close will see them as “imperfect.”

Psychological mechanism: A person discovers they possess a talent or charisma that draws admiration. The psyche adapts: to feel safe, they begin to depend on external evaluation. A cognitive distortion arises: “I am loved only for my success/image.” This grows into a life philosophy. They start to see themselves as “the chosen one,” obliged to shine for others. Thus the need for love is replaced by the need for worship.

The crisis of choice: Career or Love? The methodology describes the Geisha’s central conflict. When a partner appears who demands exclusivity, the Geisha faces a choice: the crowd or one person. The historical example of King Edward VIII, who renounced the throne for love, is a rare exception. Far more often, the Geisha chooses the career. If she sacrifices her reputation for love, she moves into the Cinderella model (serving one person). If she preserves her image but loses touch with her feelings, she may slide into the Chameleon (adjusting to the public) or, if her reputation is damaged, into Lilith (money instead of fame).

Literary and mythological context: Recall the story of Kikuya, the first female geisha. Having won her freedom, she refused to sell her body, selling only the art of conversation. It was a rebellion against the system, and at the same time, the building of a new cage. In the methodology, this is a metaphor for the price of perfection: a person can look great on the outside while carrying inside the emptiness of a restless soul. Like the samurai who sharpens his sword for years, forgetting what the war was for, the Geisha sharpens her image, forgetting what love was for.

4. 5 FAMOUS FIGURES (ARCHETYPES OF THE MODEL)

Historical and cultural figures whose behavior matched the “Geisha” model: talent, service to the public, priority of image over personal life.

Maria Callas (Greece/USA, 20th century).

Her voice was a weapon capable of breaking hearts, yet her own heart remained a fortress.

Manifestations of the model: The greatest opera singer, whose life became a textbook example of choosing art over personal happiness. Her relationship with Aristotle Onassis revealed the Geisha’s eternal conflict: when she had to choose between the stage and her lover, she hesitated, and in the end lost both. Her existence was devoted to inspiring admiration through her voice, even at the cost of her health and her solitude. She died alone, leaving behind a legend, but not a family.

Coco Chanel (France, 20th century).

She created the style of an era, yet remained outside time, and outside anyone’s embrace.

Manifestations of the model: A fashion designer who built an empire of style and became a symbol of independence. Her romances repeatedly took a back seat to her work. She created the image of a woman admired by millions, yet she herself admitted to loneliness. Her life demonstrates the priority of legacy and reputation over a traditional family. She became a brand in her own lifetime, paying for it with the right to ordinary weakness.

Marlene Dietrich (Germany/USA, 20th century).

She controlled the stage lights so carefully that no one would ever see the shadow in her eyes.

Manifestations of the model: An actress and singer who managed her image with meticulous care. Famous for the line: “I dress for the image, not for the man.” She directed men’s attention, but never allowed them to direct her. Her life was a performance in which she always remained the “untouchable star,” even in intimate relationships, preserving the distance of the Geisha.

Sarah Bernhardt (France, 19th–20th centuries).

Her death was staged as carefully as her life.

Manifestations of the model: The great actress, christened “the Divine Sarah,” known for her theatrical gestures and a life lived on display. She turned her life into a show. Even her death was choreographed. Bernhardt embodied a model where the public matters more than family. She kept male lovers, but her true love remained the stage. Her existence confirms the thesis: the Geisha belongs to art, not to a person.

Madonna (USA, 21st century).

The queen of the pop stage, who keeps changing masks in order to stay loved.

Manifestations of the model: A pop icon who endlessly reinvents her image to hold the public’s attention. Her career is built on the ability to provoke admiration and shock. Her personal relationships often became material for her art. She represents the modern version of the Geisha: the constant upkeep of an image demands the sacrifice of privacy. Her life illustrates the conviction: “The admiration of the crowd is a drug you cannot live without.”

5. EXITING THE MODEL (DEPRESSIVE AND AGGRESSIVE)

A person may face loneliness, or fight to protect their reputation. When the supply of admiration runs dry (age, a shift in the public’s taste), the crisis begins.

The depressive exit: The person realizes: “They applaud me, but they don’t love me.” The Geisha withdraws into herself. If she decides that love matters more than fame, she may move to the Cinderella model (devotion to one person) or the Chameleon (adapting to a partner). If she decides the world will never appreciate her, she may retreat into the Hippie (a rejection of standards).

The aggressive exit: The person rages against criticism. “They don’t deserve my talent.” The Geisha begins attacking the haters, defending her reputation. If the reputation is hopelessly damaged, she may shift to the Lilith model (monetizing the image, money instead of fame) or to the Puttana (shock for attention, at any price).

6. MARIA’S STORY. THE PATH OF “GEISHA”

Two years had passed since that night by the stove. Maria had accepted David’s help, though not in the way he had hoped: she did not become his woman. He delivered orders to the big city and one day took her along, found her a studio under the eaves through people he knew, and lent her money for the first month. The notebook she had filled over the winter turned into a blog about the life of a “former rebel,” and Maria began posting her story in installments, like a TV series.

People read her eagerly. They liked that she made no excuses: she wrote about Rafael, the clubs, the mountains as if she had nothing to hide. Invitations came, first to podcasts, then to speak, and she felt again that current of other people’s attention whose absence, she now saw, had chilled her in “the Community” more than the lack of firewood. There she had been free and invisible; here people looked at her, and under their gaze her back straightened.

One thing she didn’t tell her readers. In the post about the mountains the red-haired roommate was sick too, only the heroine of the blog sat at her bedside for three nights and gave her thyme tea. Maria wrote it that way the first time, almost without thinking, and that post drew more responses than any other. “You’re the real thing,” people wrote to her, and it became too late to correct it. A month later an unsigned comment appeared under the post: “But you went to the river that day with everybody else.” She deleted it without reading to the end, blocked the author, and paced the studio until evening with her heart pounding.

From then on she tended her sincerity as carefully as her lighting. She wrote her confessions in the evening and edited them in the morning, and if her voice wavered too noticeably on a recording, she shot it again. When a journalist called her confessions a well-staged performance, she took his article apart line by line in front of all her readers, and for three days they wrote him such things that he shut down his page. The victory was complete. That evening, for some reason, she couldn’t eat.

Every morning began with the phone: how many people had come overnight, how many had left, who had shared. What she would be that day, light or prickly, depended on those numbers.

David moved to the city in the second year and rented a half-basement for his workshop three blocks from her. On Saturdays he would stop by, bring groceries, fix whatever had broken during the week, and never stay long. She was grateful to him and found the gratitude a little burdensome, because around him she had to remember what she had been like in a jacket by the stove.

One of those Saturdays she was getting ready to film: she straightened the collar of her blouse, shifted the lamp, checked in the mirror how the shadow fell on her cheekbone. David stood in the doorway with a grocery bag, watching her prepare.

“Tell me honestly, aren’t you lonely?”

He asked it softly, without reproach. Maria froze with her hand at her collar.

“If I say yes, he’ll stay. And with him I’d have to be the one in the jacket, and she can’t do anything except freeze and bawl. No. Nobody gets to see me lonely. Let them think I’m unavailable.”

“I don’t have time to be lonely,” she said, and smiled the way she smiled at the camera. “Move over, you’re in the shot.”

Psychologists would call this defense through idealization: a person needs the flawless picture above all so that she herself never has to meet her own pain.

David shook his head. There was no anger in his face, only the weariness of a man who has knocked a long time and understood the door won’t open. He set the bag on the floor and went out, closing the door softly behind him.

Maria stood for a moment looking at the bag, with a bunch of herbs sticking out of it. Her phone screen lit up: “+1,000 followers.” The joy pricked her, brief and sharp, and David’s tired face slid off to the edge of her memory. She switched on the lamp and recorded the whole thing in one take.

Six months later she was at the top. She was quoted, she was invited to host evenings, and she had dresses of a kind she hadn’t owned even in her Rafael days. David no longer came by, and on Saturdays she caught herself listening for footsteps on the stairs, and then that passed too.

After one of her talks a young man came up to her, handsome, well dressed, visibly nervous. He said he had been reading her since the first post and asked whether she would have dinner with him.

“My managers will be in touch with you,” Maria answered with a smile perfected in front of the mirror.

She had no managers. He walked away, glancing back, and she told herself she had done the right thing: at dinner she would have had to be one person for two hours straight, unedited, and he might have been disappointed. Losing an admirer was out of the question; losing a man didn’t frighten her, since she didn’t have one anyway.

At home she took off her shoes and went to the mirror without turning on the overhead light. A woman loved by thousands of people looked out of the glass. Maria laid her palm against the reflection, and the glass under her palm was cold.

“They know me, they wait for me, they write to me. Only the one they love is the one who sat by a sick girl’s bed. The one who went to the river is standing right here, and nobody knows about her. I built a museum and put myself in the display case.”

She sat down on the floor, still in her dress, and thought about David, his hands that smelled of wood shavings, the jar of honey, the way he had lit the stove and never once turned to see whether she was watching. He had offered her warmth, and she had chosen light.

The phone on the table shuddered. It was David, writing for the first time in six months:

“I saw your talk. You were wonderful. But you looked very lonely. The door is still open, but not forever.”

She read it, and the tears came on their own, mascara and all, down her cheeks and onto her collar. She didn’t reach for a tissue, though it was ugly and nothing like the script.

That night she posted nothing. She sat in the dark typing a reply to him. She typed “Yes, I’m lonely” and erased it. She typed “Can I come over?” and erased that too: she pictured him seeing her up close, without the lighting, and realizing there was nothing there to love. What she feared most was that under the image there was no one.

By morning the message was still unsent. Maria washed her face, glanced at the clock, and thought there was still time to shoot a post before noon. Then she switched off the lamp and lay down facing the wall.
