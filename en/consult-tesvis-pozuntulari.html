Clinical therapy

Anxiety disorders

Anxiety, panic, phobias and fear of separation are conditions in which anxiety or fear becomes excessive and gets in the way of life. They are treated with psychotherapy and, when needed, with medication.

Book a visit

How does an anxiety disorder present?

Anxiety and fear that last a long time, cannot be controlled and interfere with ordinary life

  1. Worry you cannot switch off

    You worry about many things at once: work, family, health, everyday matters — and you cannot turn it off. There can also be “worry about the worry itself.”

  2. Panic attack

    Fear comes suddenly and peaks within a few minutes: your heart pounds, you cannot get enough air, and it feels as if you are suffocating, dying or losing control. An attack lasts 5–20 minutes.

  3. Anxiety in the body

    Your muscles are tense, you tire quickly, sleep badly, sweat, have a dry mouth and a headache.

  4. Avoidance

    You avoid places that are hard to leave or where help may not be available: transport, crowds, leaving home alone. “Safety” habits often appear: a person by your side, a phone, medication carried along.

  5. Fear of being judged

    You are very afraid that others will judge or humiliate you, and this fear does not go away. The body responds: you blush, tremble, sweat, your voice shakes.

  6. Fear of separation and silence in children

    The child is afraid to be apart from a close person or from home, refuses to sleep away from home, avoids school. Or stays stubbornly silent in some places, such as school, while speaking freely at home.

Which disorders belong to this group?

International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), section “Anxiety or fear-related disorders”, codes 6B00–6B06

  1. 6B00 · Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)

    Strong anxiety about many things that is hard to calm (“generalised” means “covering many things”). It lasts at least several months and comes with tension, fatigue and disturbed sleep.

  2. 6B01 · Panic disorder

    Panic attacks come unexpectedly and recur, and between them the fear that an attack will happen again does not go away.

  3. 6B02 · Agoraphobia

    Intense fear of situations that are hard to leave or where help may not be available, and a tendency to avoid them.

  4. 6B03 · Specific phobia

    A sharp, irrational fear of a particular object or situation and a tendency to avoid it. The person usually understands that the fear is excessive.

  5. 6B04 · Social anxiety disorder

    An intense and persistent fear that others will judge you badly or humiliate you in social situations.

  6. 6B05 · Separation anxiety disorder

    An excessive fear, not appropriate for the child’s age, of being apart from a close person (usually a parent) or from home.

  7. 6B06 · Selective mutism

    A child consistently stays silent in certain situations, such as school, while speaking normally in others, such as at home.

When should you see a doctor?

The signs a doctor uses to make a diagnosis

  • Anxiety covers many areas of life, is present most of the time for several months, and you cannot control it.
  • Along with the anxiety there is inner tension, quick tiring, difficulty concentrating, irritability, tense muscles or disturbed sleep.
  • Anxiety or fear distresses you badly or gets in the way of work, study or relationships.
  • You avoid places and situations because you fear another attack.
  • Before making a diagnosis, the doctor rules out another cause: the effect of a substance or an illness. For example, the doctor checks the thyroid and the heart and asks about caffeine and stimulants (substances that excite the nervous system).

How are anxiety disorders treated?

The basis of treatment is psychotherapy; medication is added when needed

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)

CBT is work on the thoughts and actions that keep anxiety going. In generalised anxiety this method has the most convincing evidence of benefit; usually 12–20 sessions.

  • you test how true your beliefs about anxiety really are
  • you learn to tolerate uncertainty more easily
  • exposure — gradually meeting a frightening situation together with your doctor instead of avoiding it
  • you practise solving problems
  • you learn to relax: muscle relaxation and breathing with the belly (diaphragmatic)

Exposure in agoraphobia and phobias

Gradually approaching the situations you avoid is a first-line method, that is, it is chosen first.

  • in agoraphobia — step by step you enter real situations and learn to think about them differently (reviewing anxious thoughts is called cognitive restructuring)
  • in specific phobia — a single 2–3-hour session built in steps helps with most animal and situational phobias (tied to a particular setting)
  • in fear of blood and injections — “applied tension”: you learn to tense your muscles, which stops you from fainting
  • exposure in virtual reality — a back-up option; evidence of its benefit is still being gathered

In panic disorder

CBT has a large effect; 12–16 sessions.

  • you are told how panic works (psychoeducation)
  • you learn to tolerate bodily signals — a racing heart, shortness of breath — without expecting disaster (interoceptive exposure)
  • you gradually enter real situations
  • you review catastrophic thoughts

In social anxiety

The first-line treatment is individual CBT, 12–16 sessions. It works better than group CBT.

  • you learn to turn your attention from yourself to what is happening around you
  • you watch a recording of yourself from the outside (video feedback)
  • you test your fears in practice (behavioural experiments)
  • you review your thoughts and gradually enter frightening situations

In children

CBT with parental involvement and a behavioural approach.

  • in separation anxiety, parents’ involvement in CBT is very important; the return to school goes step by step
  • frequent calls and visits, when a parent adapts to the child’s anxiety (accommodation), strengthen the symptoms
  • in selective mutism the child is led step by step toward speech and a stranger is gradually introduced; the doctor agrees the plan with the teacher
  • the parent stops speaking for the child

Medication

The first choice is antidepressants of the SSRI and SNRI groups (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors).

  • the effect is assessed after 4–6 weeks; a full response may come after 8–12 weeks
  • treatment starts with a small dose; in the first days anxiety may briefly increase
  • benzodiazepines (tranquillisers) — only in the acute period and for no more than 2–4 weeks: it is easy to become dependent on them
  • after remission (when the symptoms have gone) treatment continues for at least 12 months; it is stopped gradually — stopping suddenly raises the risk of anxiety returning

Lifestyle

An addition to the main treatment.

  • less caffeine
  • regular physical activity
  • healthy sleep habits (sleep hygiene)
  • avoiding alcohol and psychoactive substances

Frequently asked questions

Is anxiety a normal feeling or an illness?

There is a clinical difference between ordinary anxiety and a disorder: how strong the anxiety is, how long it lasts, whether it can be controlled, how it shows in the body and whether it interferes with life. A diagnosis is made by specific criteria.

Can you stop worrying by willpower?

No. Generalised anxiety disorder is a disturbance of regulation at the level of the brain (neurobiological dysregulation). That is why CBT or medication, which act at this level, help.

Are benzodiazepines the main treatment?

No. They are used only briefly, for 2–4 weeks, because the risk of dependence is high. The first choice is antidepressants of the SSRI and SNRI groups.

Aren’t antidepressants only for depression?

No. SSRIs and SNRIs also help in anxiety disorders: the serotonin system influences the activity of the amygdala (a small area of the brain).

Will I have to take antidepressants for life?

No. Once the symptoms have gone, treatment continues for 12 months, and then it can be stopped gradually. If anxiety returns, treatment is restarted.
