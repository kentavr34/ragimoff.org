1. Definition and nosology

Rumination-Regurgitation Disorder (ICD-11: 6B85; DSM-5-TR: F98.21) — characterized by voluntary or semi-voluntary regurgitation of swallowed food followed by re-chewing, re-swallowing, or spitting out. It is not fully explained by a medical condition (gastroesophageal reflux or other GI disease).

2. History

  • The term ‘merycism’ was used in ancient times.
  • DSM-IV — As an infant and childhood disorder.
  • DSM-5 (2013) — diagnosis at all ages.

3. Epidemiology

  • Prevalence: rare in infants and children; 6–10% in patients with intellectual disability; increasing recognition in adults (especially comorbid with eating disorders).
  • Comorbidity: ID, ASD, anxiety, BN, BED.

4. Etiology and pathogenesis

  • Behavioral conditioning — ‘post-prandial belching’ followed by regurgitation as a learned behavior.
  • Stress and anxiety.
  • In ID/ASD, self-stimulation with food.

5. Clinical features

  • Regurgitation minutes to hours after food intake.
  • Eating disorder outcomes: weight loss, malnutrition, electrolyte imbalance, dental erosion, halitosis.
  • Social consequences: shame, social isolation.

6. Diagnosis

6.1 Unified diagnostic criteria

A. Recurrent regurgitation of swallowed food — DSM-5-TR: ≥ 1 month; ICD-11: several times a week over at least several weeks; re-chewing, re-swallowing, or spitting out.

B. Regurgitation is not related to a medical condition (e.g., reflux, pyloric stenosis, duodenal obstruction).

C. Does not occur in the context of anorexia, bulimia, BED, ARFID.

D. If occurring in the context of another mental disorder (especially ID, ASD), significant severity requires separate assessment.

6.2 Differences between sources

  • DSM-5-TR / ICD-11 — criteria overlap; the duration and the age threshold differ: DSM-5-TR requires ≥ 1 month, ICD-11 requires several times a week over at least several weeks and a developmental age of ≥ 2 years.

6.3 Diagnostic algorithm

  1. Clinical interview.
  2. Medical assessment — exclusion of GERD, pyloric pathology, gastroparesis (upper endoscopy, motility study).
  3. Manometry — confirmation of functional regurgitation.
  4. Weight and nutritional status.
  5. Comorbid eating disorder screening.

7. Examination and assessment

  • Endoscopy, manometry, pH-monitoring.
  • Complete blood count, electrolytes.

8. Treatment

  1. Diaphragmatic breathing — first-line behavioral intervention (Chitkara D.K. et al. Am J Gastroenterol 2006; effect 50–70%).
  2. Habit reversal — learning alternative behavior.
  3. Biofeedback.
  4. Stress management and CBT.
  5. In ID/ASD, functional behavior analysis and ABA.
  6. Medical: GERD: proton pump inhibitor as initial intervention.

8.1 Treatment methods

  1. Diaphragmatic Breathing — Slow abdominal breathing for 5–10 minutes after eating — prevents regurgitation.
  2. Habit Reversal Training (HRT — Habit Reversal Training) — Developing alternative behaviors.
  3. Biofeedback — Abdominal muscle control.
  4. FBA — functional behavior analysis (in ID/ASD) — Analysis of behavior trigger and maintainer.

8.2 Differences between sources

  • Rome Foundation criteria (functional GI).
  • NICE NG69 — general eating disorder principles.

9. Prognosis

  • Significant improvement with diaphragmatic breathing and habit reversal.

10. Myths and misconceptions

Myth 1: “Rumination is a form of GERD”

Evidence: distinct disorder; manometry and clinical model differ.

Myth 2: “Only medical surgery (Nissen) solves it”

Evidence: Nissen fundoplication ineffective for functional rumination and contraindicated; behavioral intervention first-line.

Myth 3: “The patient behaves ‘volitionally’, requires discipline”

Evidence: It is a semi-voluntary behavior, based on conditioning; punishment is ineffective.

Myth 4: “Antiemetics are effective”

Evidence: Antiemetics (e.g., ondansetron) are ineffective in rumination.

Myth 5: “Cannot be discontinued in a patient with ID/ASD”

Evidence: Behavioral interventions are effective; active intervention is essential to prevent nutritional complications.
